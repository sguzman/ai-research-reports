# Aesthetics for the Maimed

> **Editorial note.** “Liberal gothic” is a proposed polemical label, not an established scholarly genre. This essay preserves an intentionally severe voice while separating rhetorical interpretation from empirical claims. It does **not** claim that liberalism uniquely produces suffering, that creators secretly coordinate to preserve trauma, or that all art about injury is exploitative. Its narrower question is whether some contemporary cultural systems can turn recurring injury, confession, alienation, and therapeutic self-description into durable aesthetic and commercial forms—and what would distinguish diagnosis from exploitation or restoration.

## The Wound as Cultural Form

There is a recognizable contemporary aesthetic in which the wound arrives before the world.

The protagonist is not first a citizen, lover, worker, believer, builder, rival, parent, coward, hero, fool, or creature of appetite. He is first what happened to him. The narrative does not discover injury as one fact among many; injury becomes the key that unlocks the person. A childhood event explains the adult. A diagnosis explains the temperament. An exclusion explains the politics. A humiliation explains the desire. A trauma explains the plot.

This is not imaginary. Trauma has become a major formal and thematic problem in contemporary literature. Anne Whitehead’s *Trauma Fiction* describes how modern novelists do not merely write **about** trauma but alter chronology, repetition, memory, and narrative form in trying to represent it. Literary criticism has consequently developed a large vocabulary for texts organized around damaged memory, fractured identity, belated recognition, and testimony. Andrew Smith’s work on Gothic fiction after the First World War likewise shows that Gothic structures have long been used to represent psychologically damaged selves and historical catastrophe. The combination of haunting and injury is therefore not new, and certainly not the invention of a twenty-first-century streaming executive. [Anne Whitehead, *Trauma Fiction*](https://edinburghuniversitypress.com/book-trauma-fiction.html) [Andrew Smith, *Gothic Fiction and the Writing of Trauma, 1914–1934*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/gothic-fiction-and-the-writing-of-trauma-19141934/A8EBE22E247944FD4A382BEA5CCFE888)

Yet a form can be old and still become newly dominant. The suspicion behind the phrase **liberal gothic** is that a particular descendant of the trauma narrative has spread beyond Gothic fiction into prestige television, memoir, games, online confession, criticism, branding, and political storytelling. It is Gothic stripped of the ancestral castle but not of haunting. The specter is now the self’s unresolved history. The curse is inherited damage. The dungeon is isolation. The monster is sometimes an institution, sometimes a family, sometimes the body, sometimes memory, sometimes nothing more dramatic than a life that never coheres.

The name is deliberately tendentious. It points toward a cultural milieu in which therapeutic language, recognition, inclusion, vulnerability, self-narration, and suspicion of inherited authority often carry moral prestige. But the adjective **liberal** must not do illicit causal work. An aesthetic can be common in progressive-coded institutions without being caused by progressive ideology; it can be used by conservatives, apolitical creators, advertisers, churches, populists, influencers, and anyone else who discovers that pain is narratively adhesive. If the category cannot survive that distinction, it is propaganda rather than criticism.

The stronger proposition is therefore not “liberals love wounds.” It is this:

**When a culture becomes highly skilled at naming, representing, circulating, and monetizing injury, it may become better at keeping wounds culturally active than at distinguishing diagnosis, exploitation, and restoration.**

That is a claim worth testing because it does not require a villain in a room. It requires only incentives, selection, imitation, and an audience whose attention is finite.

## What the Age Actually Produces

The original temptation is to say that modernity manufactures broken people as deliberately as a factory manufactures bolts. That is too easy. It confuses a causal system with a conscious manufacturer and turns a difficult empirical problem into moral theater.

There is, however, no need to pretend that widespread social disconnection is fictional. The World Health Organization’s 2025 Commission on Social Connection estimated that roughly **one in six people globally** experience loneliness, with particularly high prevalence among adolescents and young adults. The same report describes multiple drivers: health conditions, marginalization, low income and education, living alone, bereavement, job loss, relationship breakdown, migration, and other life changes. It also places rapid urbanization, demographic change, digital transformation, and inequality among the broader conditions reshaping social connection. [WHO Commission on Social Connection, 2025](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/978240112360) [WHO Q&A on social connection](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/social-connection)

This is already grim enough. It does not need the embellishment that a single ideology engineered loneliness.

A civilization can produce recurrent forms of injury without anyone designing the whole. Housing patterns can separate kin. Labor markets can reward mobility and punish rootedness. Digital systems can make comparison continuous. Economic insecurity can make commitment costly. Family structures can change. Religious participation can decline. Medical systems can become better at naming distress. Communities can become simultaneously more tolerant and less intimate. None of these mechanisms implies that “the system” possesses one intention.

The distinction matters because the rhetoric of manufacture can otherwise swallow causality. If every wound is attributed to one civilizational machine, then no evidence can discriminate among explanations. The concept becomes invincible by becoming empty.

A better indictment is colder: **complex systems can repeatedly generate human outcomes that no actor intended, while institutions can subsequently learn to manage, classify, sell to, or politically organize around those outcomes.** That is not a conspiracy theory. It is ordinary institutional adaptation.

There is also a symmetry that the polemic must accept. Earlier societies were not factories of wholeness. Thick communities could provide belonging and also coercion. Stable families could protect and also brutalize. Religious order could orient and also persecute. Sexual norms could discipline desire and also imprison people in violent households. Local rootedness could create reciprocity and also inherited exclusion. The past is not a control group labeled HEALTH.

If “liberal gothic” is to mean anything, it cannot be nostalgia with stage lighting. Its target is not modern suffering as such. Its target is a specific **relationship to suffering**: the conversion of injury into a durable representational and attention economy.

## The Market Does Not Need a Conspiracy

The market is more frightening when it is not a person.

A person can be shamed. An incentive cannot. A producer can possess motives; a selection environment merely keeps what performs.

This distinction removes one of the weakest claims in the original indictment: that writers, filmmakers, journalists, platforms, therapists, or brands must secretly **want** people to remain wounded because their careers depend on it. That accusation exceeds the evidence and is usually unnecessary. A culture can overproduce certain forms without its participants sharing a plan.

Social-media research supplies a cleaner mechanism. Brady and colleagues examined more than 12 million tweets from 7,331 users and conducted preregistered experiments. They found that positive social feedback for expressions of moral outrage predicted more outrage expression later, while network norms also shaped what users expressed. The result is a reinforcement process: people can learn what receives reward and adapt without ever signing a manifesto in favor of permanent anger. [Brady et al., *Science Advances*, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8363141/)

A 2025 preregistered audit of Twitter’s engagement-based ranking found something similarly impersonal. Relative to a reverse-chronological baseline, engagement optimization amplified more partisan and out-group-hostile political content and more negative emotion, including anger, sadness, and anxiety. The algorithm did not need to “hate” anyone. It selected for engagement, and emotional divisiveness emerged as part of what that objective rewarded. [Milli et al., *PNAS Nexus*, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11894805/)

This is the useful model for the culture market of wounds.

One does not need an executive saying, “Keep them sick.” One needs a system in which injury is narratively legible, confession is cheap to produce, emotional intensity improves retention, recognizable identities reduce marketing uncertainty, and successful forms are copied. Under those conditions, the wound can reproduce itself as **format** even when every individual creator believes he is helping.

The market can feed on wounds without having created them for food.

This is a much stronger accusation because it can be investigated. Which emotional themes receive disproportionate promotion? Which forms generate completion, sharing, subscription, prestige, or critical attention? Do works organized around trauma outperform otherwise similar works? Are recovery narratives less marketable than recurring crisis? Do platforms amplify the most dysregulating variants of a theme? These are empirical questions. They can yield “yes,” “no,” or “sometimes.”

A cultural theory that cannot tolerate “no” is merely a temperament with citations.

## The Culture Market of Wounds

The phrase **culture market of wounds** therefore names an incentive pattern, not a cabal.

Pain is excellent narrative material because pain supplies stakes. Loss generates memory. Shame generates secrecy. Injury generates causality. Estrangement generates desire. Trauma gives the writer a ready-made before and after. It can explain behavior, create sympathy, justify nonlinear form, and produce revelation. No sinister motive is required to see why artists return to it.

The problem begins when injury ceases to be material and becomes **automatic authority**.

Parul Sehgal’s 2022 essay “The Case Against the Trauma Plot” attacks precisely this reduction. Her criticism is not that trauma is unreal or unworthy of art; it is that trauma can become a narrative master key that reduces a character to symptoms of a past event and converts mystery into diagnosis. The plot that promises depth can become mechanistic: show the wound, explain the person. [Parul Sehgal, “The Case Against the Trauma Plot,” *The New Yorker*, 2022](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2022/01/03/the-case-against-the-trauma-plot)

That aesthetic shortcut matters because it is easily industrialized. A wound is recognizable across a trailer, blurb, thumbnail, pitch deck, award campaign, creator monologue, or fifteen-second clip. It converts messy life into a compact signal. It tells the audience what register of seriousness is being offered.

At its worst, the result is not tragedy but **managed recurrence**. The narrative performs recognition without changing the conditions that make recognition necessary. It gives the sufferer a mirror, then another mirror, then a premium mirror with better cinematography. It becomes a sequence of culturally sanctioned returns to the same injury.

Here the polemical charge “necrotic” becomes useful if it is defined rather than shouted.

A work is not necrotic because it is sad. It is not necrotic because it depicts abuse, illness, loneliness, prejudice, suicide, grief, or failure. It is not necrotic because the protagonist does not recover. Some wounds do not close. Some tragedies are tragic precisely because restoration is unavailable.

A cultural form becomes **necrotic** when it repeatedly converts injury into attention, status, or revenue while systematically weakening the audience’s ability to distinguish among cause, agency, treatment, adaptation, political remedy, and irreversible loss. Necrosis is not pain. It is circulation without metabolism.

That criterion is severe enough to condemn something and narrow enough to be falsifiable.

## Liberal Gothic as a Proposed Mode

The Gothic has always known that the past returns.

Old Gothic architecture made memory spatial: ancestral crimes lived in walls, bloodlines, chambers, portraits, ruins, monasteries, crypts. Modern trauma fiction often internalizes the architecture. The haunted house becomes the haunted self. Memory becomes the corridor. The locked room becomes the event that cannot be integrated. The ghost becomes a recurrence in perception, behavior, or narrative structure.

Andrew Smith’s work on World War I Gothic makes the historical continuity explicit: ghost stories and uncanny forms were used to represent soldiers who seemed psychologically absent, dislocated, or unable to return cleanly to prewar selves. Gothic narrative could both contain trauma and reveal the failure of containment. [Smith, *Gothic Fiction and the Writing of Trauma*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/gothic-fiction-and-the-writing-of-trauma-19141934/A8EBE22E247944FD4A382BEA5CCFE888)

So what, if anything, is new in “liberal gothic”?

Not trauma. Not grief. Not the wounded protagonist. Not even therapeutic language in the broad sense.

The proposed novelty lies in a **moral-aesthetic package**:

- injury is treated as privileged access to authenticity;
- self-narration is treated as a principal route to recognition;
- institutions and inherited norms are approached first through the harms they can conceal;
- moral seriousness is signaled through attention to vulnerability, exclusion, identity, mental health, and damaged intimacy;
- ambiguity and fracture often appear more credible than flourishing or stable form;
- the narrative’s ethical achievement can consist in making pain legible even when it offers no account of cure.

This is a hypothesis about a mode, not a census of culture. It may describe *A Little Life* better than an action franchise, a confessional prestige drama better than a romantic comedy, a narrative game about grief better than a competitive shooter. It is not supposed to explain everything.

The label also needs a negative control. If a right-wing martyr narrative, evangelical testimony, nationalist grievance film, manosphere confession, or populist outrage feed uses the same structure—wound, recognition, moral authority, recurrence—then the **wound form** is broader than liberalism. The political code changes; the attention logic remains.

That symmetry is not a concession. It is a test.

## The Creative Class Without Mind-Reading

The creative class is an easy villain because it speaks for a living.

It is tempting to accuse writers, filmmakers, critics, journalists, therapists, and influencers of cultivating damage because damage gives them material. But this collapses several distinct questions.

Does a creator earn money from depicting suffering? Often, yes.

Does a critic gain status by interpreting suffering? Sometimes.

Does a platform gain engagement from emotional intensity? There is direct evidence that engagement systems can amplify negative and divisive material.

Does any of this prove that the people involved intend suffering to persist? No.

The distinction is not moral squeamishness. It is the difference between analysis and mind-reading.

A journalist can sincerely believe that testimony matters and still work inside incentives that reward emotionally intense stories. A filmmaker can genuinely want to represent depression and still reproduce a prestige convention in which damage substitutes for characterization. An influencer can experience real distress and also learn that public distress produces attention. A therapist can help patients while therapeutic vocabulary migrates into marketing. None of these contradictions requires fraud.

Institutional criticism should therefore ask what **roles and incentives** do, not what souls secretly desire.

The harshest legitimate criticism is this: a professional culture can become so accustomed to mediation that it mistakes mediation for remedy. Naming, representing, validating, contextualizing, and platforming are all real acts. They may be humane. They are not the same act as reducing the underlying injury.

The broker of pain is not necessarily a vampire. But a civilization full of brokers can still forget the difference between **making suffering legible** and **making less suffering**.

That is where the indictment belongs.

## Symmetry Check: Negativity Is Not Liberal Property

Any theory called “liberal gothic” risks cheating by selecting its evidence from one ideological tribe.

The available evidence warns against that.

Bellovary, Young, and Goldenberg analyzed 140,358 tweets from 44 left- and right-leaning news organizations. Negative affect was more common than positive affect, and greater negativity predicted greater engagement. Crucially, the study found no meaningful ideological difference in the use of negative affect or in its relationship with engagement. [Bellovary, Young & Goldenberg, *Affective Science*, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8364833/)

That result should be allowed to wound the theory.

If negativity, grievance, anger, threat, humiliation, and emotionally adhesive conflict perform across ideological orientations, then the market incentive is not liberal. The broader mechanism is **attention under competition**. Different political cultures provide different objects of injury and different moral grammars, but neither side owns the wound.

The right has its own Gothic repertoire: civilizational betrayal, national humiliation, demographic replacement, emasculation, religious persecution, lost greatness, abandoned veterans, dead towns, stolen elections, mutilated tradition. These narratives can be every bit as haunted, identity-forming, repetitive, and commercially useful as progressive narratives of trauma, exclusion, and structural injury.

The symmetry check therefore forces a revision.

“Liberal gothic” can remain a useful aesthetic label only if it refers to **specific codes of presentation**—therapeutic introspection, recognition, vulnerability, institutional suspicion, confessional legitimacy—not to the universal fact that political and commercial storytellers exploit negative emotion.

The moment the theory says only liberals aestheticize wounds, the evidence defeats it.

Good. A theory that cannot be defeated is not worth preserving.

## Diagnostic, Necrotic, Restorative

The related Liberal Gothic briefing in this corpus proposed a useful three-way distinction: **diagnostic**, **necrotic**, and **restorative** culture. The terms are worth retaining, but only as analytic categories rather than verdicts assigned by taste.

### Diagnostic culture

Diagnostic culture makes an injury visible.

It names loneliness, abuse, grief, exploitation, humiliation, disability, exclusion, addiction, or psychological fracture. Its value is epistemic: a person or social condition that had been ignored becomes describable. Diagnosis can itself be liberating because unnamed suffering is difficult to contest.

The danger is obvious. Diagnosis can become identity. A vocabulary developed to describe a condition can become the only vocabulary through which the person understands himself.

### Necrotic culture

Necrotic culture keeps the wound culturally productive without improving understanding or expanding agency.

Its characteristic motion is repetition. The same injury returns because it reliably produces emotion, status, engagement, or moral clarity. Causality narrows. Characters become symptoms. Audiences become demographic wounds. The form learns how to reproduce recognition more efficiently than it learns how to distinguish recovery from recurrence.

Necrosis does not require cynical creators. It can be an emergent property of incentives.

### Restorative culture

Restorative culture does not require happy endings.

It expands agency, causal understanding, social connection, moral vocabulary, or the capacity to live with irreversible loss. Sometimes restoration is cure. Sometimes it is integration. Sometimes it is tragedy understood without self-deception.

This category matters because otherwise the critique degenerates into “art should be cheerful.” That would be idiotic. A civilization that forbids darkness is not healthy; it is anesthetized.

The question is not whether art enters the wound. The question is what it can do there.

## Counterevidence: The Wound Can Teach

A serious indictment must include the evidence most inconvenient to it.

Fiction is not merely a machine for preserving pathology. A 2023 systematic review of 16 empirical studies found that reading fiction and watching fictional films can influence social cognition, including empathy and theory-of-mind processes, depending on the audience and intervention; effects were especially plausible when fiction was paired with discussion or other activities. The literature remains heterogeneous, but the existence of such effects is enough to destroy the blanket proposition that emotionally difficult fiction is socially useless. [Rezende & Shigaeff, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10715231/)

Trauma narratives can also connect private injury to social structures rather than privatize everything. Trauma studies has long examined testimony, memory, war, racism, violence, historical catastrophe, and the difficulty of representing experiences that exceed ordinary narrative form. The genre can expose precisely what the harsher polemic claims culture hides.

Even Gothic itself has restorative possibilities. Smith’s study of postwar Gothic describes stories in which home, family, romance, memory, and the supernatural become ways to imagine whether a damaged soldier can return to social life. Gothic does not merely decorate the wound. It can stage the problem of reintegration.

This matters because the original polemic’s easiest move was to equate art about pain with appetite for pain.

That move fails.

The presence of a wound in a work tells us almost nothing by itself. The same subject can be diagnostic, necrotic, restorative, exploitative, comic, tragic, propagandistic, therapeutic, or aesthetically autonomous. The classification depends on form, context, incentives, audience, and consequences—not on a keyword search for “trauma.”

A culture incapable of depicting suffering would be morally blind. A culture capable only of depicting suffering would be spiritually exhausted.

Those are different failures.

## Why “Cure” Is a Dangerous Word

The original essay demanded cure with the fury of a civilization tired of symptom management. That fury should be retained. The word should not be left unexamined.

“Cure” sounds clean until the object is a human life.

Some injuries are diseases. Some are injustices. Some are griefs. Some are conflicts among goods. Some are differences that another faction would like to medicalize. Some are consequences of choices. Some are irreversible facts. Some are identities people value. Some are political categories. Some are ordinary tragedy.

A culture that worships cure can become monstrous if it assumes agreement about what a healed human being must be.

This is the necessary limit on the rhetoric of wholeness. Words such as order, discipline, adulthood, family, beauty, health, nobility, and strength name real goods, but they are not self-interpreting. They can orient a civilization; they can also be used to conceal domination. A serious restorative politics must specify which capacities it wants to increase and whose coercive power is required to do so.

The answer cannot simply be “traditional form.” Tradition contains accumulated knowledge and accumulated injury.

Nor can the answer simply be “validation.” Validation can prevent cruelty and still become an endpoint that never asks what happens next.

The interesting political problem lies between them: **how to build forms strong enough to sustain attachment and agency without making inherited form immune to challenge.**

That is more difficult than either therapeutic permissiveness or authoritarian nostalgia. It also has the virtue of being a real problem.

## What Would Make the Indictment True?

A stylized essay is allowed to be excessive. A serious thesis must still expose its throat.

The “culture market of wounds” hypothesis would gain support if several patterns appeared together:

1. trauma-, grievance-, or injury-centered content systematically received more promotion or engagement than comparable alternatives;
2. the advantage persisted after controlling for genre, fame, production budget, platform, and audience composition;
3. institutions repeatedly favored recurring crisis and self-disclosure over narratives that increased agency or resolved the same problems;
4. producers adapted content toward the injury features that performed best;
5. audiences learned the same incentive and increased wound-centered self-presentation in response to social reward;
6. the pattern appeared across media markets rather than in a curated handful of prestige examples.

Some pieces already exist. Social-feedback studies show reinforcement of outrage expression. Algorithm audits show amplification of divisive emotional content. Literary criticism identifies trauma plots and formulaic uses of injury. None of these, individually or together, proves that “modern culture requires the broken.” They establish mechanisms from which a narrower theory can be built.

The thesis would weaken if trauma-centered content did not outperform alternatives, if restorative narratives were equally or more rewarded, if the proposed “liberal gothic” features were evenly distributed across ideological and cultural milieus, or if audiences exposed to difficult narratives reliably gained understanding, empathy, connection, and agency rather than recurring identification with injury.

Again: good. Let the thesis bleed.

If it cannot survive evidence, it deserves to die.

## What Remains of the Indictment

After stripping away the mind-reading, nostalgia, ideological asymmetry, and totalizing causal claims, something hard remains.

Modern institutions are extraordinarily capable of **representation**.

They can measure loneliness, classify symptoms, name forms of exclusion, produce memoirs, commission documentaries, construct prestige dramas, create support vocabularies, personalize feeds, discover communities, target advertising, moderate discourse, and convert almost any recurrent human difficulty into a searchable category.

This capacity is morally ambiguous.

Legibility can save people. It can also make them marketable.

Recognition can end isolation. It can also stabilize identity around the condition being recognized.

Representation can expose a hidden injustice. It can also substitute for institutional change.

Therapeutic language can help someone understand a wound. It can also become a consumer dialect.

Narrative can metabolize suffering. It can also recirculate it.

The strongest version of **Aesthetics for the Maimed** is therefore not that the artist is a vampire and the wounded are livestock. That image is memorable and often false.

The stronger version is more impersonal and more disturbing:

**A society can become excellent at turning suffering into information, identity, culture, and engagement while remaining mediocre at creating the material and relational conditions under which less suffering is produced.**

No conspiracy is necessary. No class must secretly desire misery. The mismatch can arise from specialization. The artist represents. The platform ranks. The advertiser targets. The clinician treats. The bureaucrat categorizes. The critic interprets. The audience clicks. Each actor can perform his local function rationally while the whole system becomes magnificent at circulation and weak at restoration.

That is the machine worth fearing.

Not because it hates health.

Because it may have no variable called health in the objective function.

## Conclusion: Cure or Circulation

There is still something obscene in a culture that can render every wound beautifully and cannot say what a less wounded life would require.

The obscenity is not the depiction of pain. Pain deserves witnesses. The obscenity begins when witness becomes a complete moral economy: when naming substitutes for changing, when recognition substitutes for agency, when recurrence substitutes for development, when a person’s damage becomes the most reliable explanation of who he is, when an institution’s sensitivity becomes evidence of virtue regardless of what happens downstream.

This is where the old severity returns.

A civilization should be able to ask more of art than consolation. More of politics than recognition. More of markets than extraction. More of therapy than vocabulary. More of communities than tolerance at a distance. More of adults than permanent self-explanation.

But it must also ask more of the critic.

The critic does not get to call every sorrow decadence. He does not get to infer conspiracy from incentive, liberalism from introspection, exploitation from representation, or health from whatever social order he happens to admire. If he wishes to accuse a culture of feeding on wounds, he must show the feeding mechanism. If he wishes to call a form necrotic, he must distinguish it from diagnosis and restoration. If he claims the wound is being preserved, he must identify what plausible closure would look like.

Otherwise he too becomes a merchant of injury—only his commodity is disgust.

So the term **liberal gothic** survives, but under discipline.

It names a proposed contemporary style in which haunted interiority, therapeutic recognition, damaged intimacy, institutional suspicion, vulnerability, and moralized injury converge. It does not name all liberal art. It does not prove a liberal monopoly on negativity. It does not establish that creators want audiences sick. It does not turn the past into a lost paradise of whole people.

Its real use is diagnostic: to notice when the wound has become not merely subject matter but architecture.

And the final question remains brutal enough without fantasy:

Does the work help metabolize the wound—or merely keep it circulating?

That is where the aesthetic judgment becomes a civilizational one.
