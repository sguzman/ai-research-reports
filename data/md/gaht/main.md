# Feminizing Gender-Affirming Hormone Therapy: Effects, Risks, and Evidence by Age

## Abstract

Feminizing gender-affirming hormone therapy (GAHT) uses estrogen, with testosterone suppression when needed, to induce or maintain physical traits that are more congruent with a transfeminine person's treatment goals. In adults, the expected direction of many physical changes is well established: breast development, increased fat mass, reduced lean mass and muscle strength, reduced sebum production, and suppression of testicular function. The magnitude and timing of those changes vary substantially, and hormone therapy does not reverse all traits established by prior testosterone-driven puberty, such as voice deepening or much of adult skeletal geometry.

The evidence is less certain for long-term clinical outcomes. Observational studies identify elevated venous thromboembolism and stroke rates in transfeminine populations relative to some reference groups, but treatment regimens, age, smoking, socioeconomic conditions, and other cardiovascular risks complicate causal attribution. Fertility can be impaired substantially, but infertility should not be described as inevitable or reliably permanent. Adult bone mineral density is usually maintained when adequate sex-steroid exposure is present, whereas puberty suppression during adolescence can reduce bone-density accrual while treatment continues.

Evidence about medical intervention in adolescents is materially less certain than evidence about the direction of induced physical changes. Systematic reviews published in 2024 and 2025 found that puberty suppression reliably suppresses puberty and that feminizing or masculinizing hormones induce secondary sex characteristics, but evidence for many psychological, cognitive, fertility, bone, growth, and cardiometabolic outcomes remains low or very low certainty. Professional guidelines and public health systems have reached different policy conclusions from this evidence. Those policy decisions should be reported separately from empirical findings.

This review is descriptive rather than a prescribing protocol. Dose selection, laboratory targets, monitoring, fertility counseling, and contraindication management require individualized clinical judgment and current local guidance.

## 1. Scope and Terminology

The focus is feminizing GAHT for people whose endogenous puberty or gonadal function is predominantly testosterone-driven. The term includes estrogen therapy and, when clinically needed to achieve treatment goals, a medication or intervention that reduces testosterone production or action. Puberty suppression with gonadotropin-releasing hormone analogues (GnRHa) is a related but distinct intervention and is discussed separately because its evidence base and developmental implications differ from those of adult feminizing hormone therapy.

Several distinctions prevent common errors in this literature:

1. **Physical efficacy and long-term health outcomes are different questions.** Estrogen can reliably induce secondary sex characteristics even when evidence about decades-long cardiovascular, cancer, fertility, or neurocognitive outcomes remains limited.
2. **Adults and adolescents should not be pooled casually.** Adult treatment usually occurs after endogenous puberty; adolescent treatment may alter the course of pubertal development itself.
3. **Puberty suppression and GAHT are not the same intervention.** Studies of adolescents often include both sequentially, making it difficult to attribute later outcomes to one component.
4. **Clinical guidelines are recommendations, not direct measurements of treatment effect.** They combine evidence with judgments about benefits, harms, values, feasibility, and acceptable uncertainty.
5. **Law and commissioning policy are not evidence grades.** A jurisdiction may permit, restrict, or decline to fund a treatment for reasons that extend beyond the underlying clinical studies.

## 2. Current Guideline Context

The Endocrine Society continues to list its 2017 clinical practice guideline on gender dysphoria/gender incongruence as its relevant guideline resource. It recommends specialist assessment, counseling about fertility preservation before hormonal treatment, evaluation of conditions that could be worsened by sex-steroid therapy, and ongoing clinical and laboratory monitoring. For adolescents, it recommends management by an expert multidisciplinary team and distinguishes pubertal suppression from later sex-hormone treatment.

The World Professional Association for Transgender Health (WPATH) published Standards of Care Version 8 (SOC8) in 2022. Its hormone-therapy chapter recommends individualized assessment and monitoring, counseling about fertility preservation, avoidance of ethinyl estradiol for gender-affirming estrogen therapy, and use of transdermal estrogen in people considered at higher venous-thromboembolism risk when estrogen therapy is otherwise indicated.

These documents remain influential but should not be treated as proof that every recommendation rests on high-certainty evidence. Guideline recommendations can be strong even where the evidence base is observational because the recommendation also reflects clinical experience, feasibility, patient values, and the harms of alternative courses of action.

## 3. Physical Effects in Adults

### 3.1 Breast development

Breast development is a predictable effect of feminizing estrogen therapy, but final size is highly variable. A prospective Amsterdam cohort using three-dimensional imaging followed 69 adult transgender women for three years. Breast volume continued to increase over the observation period, although the median development remained modest and individual satisfaction varied. Such data contradict two simplistic claims: that breast development is complete within a few months, and that a particular Tanner stage or cup size can be promised from a standard regimen.

Progestogens are sometimes added with the goal of increasing breast development, but a systematic review found insufficient evidence to establish a reliable breast-development benefit and identified uncertainty about potential harms. They therefore should not be presented in a general review as a proven routine component of feminizing therapy.

### 3.2 Body composition and muscle

Estrogen therapy with testosterone suppression generally increases fat mass and reduces lean mass and muscle strength. Fat distribution often shifts toward a pattern more typical of estrogen-dominant physiology. The extent of change depends on age, genetics, baseline body composition, activity, treatment duration, and the achieved hormonal environment.

These soft-tissue changes should be distinguished from skeletal changes. Hormone therapy begun after completion of testosterone-driven puberty does not meaningfully shorten adult height, reverse an already deepened voice, or remodel the adult shoulder and facial skeleton into a prepubertal baseline. Changes in body fat, muscle, skin, hair growth, and breast tissue can alter appearance substantially without erasing prior skeletal development.

### 3.3 Skin, hair, and sexual function

Reduced androgen activity commonly decreases sebum production and can reduce acne and slow body-hair growth. Existing terminal facial hair often persists and may require separate cosmetic treatment if removal is desired. Androgen-sensitive scalp-hair loss may slow, while regrowth of already lost hair is variable.

Testosterone suppression commonly reduces spontaneous erections, libido for some individuals, testicular volume, and spermatogenesis. These outcomes are variable and should not be converted into a promise of complete erectile loss or sterility.

## 4. Fertility

Fertility effects are clinically important because they can be substantial and because reversibility is uncertain. A 2025 systematic review of reproductive effects reported testicular atrophy, seminiferous-tubule changes, hyalinization or fibrosis, and impaired spermatogenesis after estrogen-based GAHT. At the same time, the literature contains evidence of residual germ cells and reports of fertility recovery or successful reproduction after treatment interruption in some individuals.

The defensible conclusion is therefore narrower than the inherited report's statement that most people will become infertile. Estrogen-based GAHT can markedly impair sperm production, and the ability to recover fertility after prolonged treatment cannot be guaranteed. This uncertainty is why both the Endocrine Society and WPATH recommend discussing fertility-preservation options before treatment when future genetic parenthood may matter to the patient.

Puberty suppression introduces a separate issue: a person who begins suppression early in puberty may never develop mature sperm before later GAHT. That can limit conventional sperm cryopreservation options. Evidence for experimental alternatives such as testicular-tissue preservation remains limited and should not be described as established fertility preservation.

## 5. Cardiovascular and Thromboembolic Outcomes

### 5.1 What the population evidence shows

Cardiovascular evidence is largely observational. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of 22 studies found that, compared with cisgender men, transgender women had a pooled relative risk of about 1.3 for stroke and 2.2 for venous thromboembolism; myocardial-infarction risk was not significantly different in that comparison. Absolute event incidence varied across cohorts, and the studies differed in age, treatment era, estrogen formulation, route, smoking, comorbidity, and comparator population.

A 2025 systematic review likewise found a higher occurrence of venous thrombosis among transgender women receiving hormone therapy than among cisgender male comparators. These studies support treating thromboembolic risk as real rather than hypothetical, but they do not establish a single risk multiplier applicable to every estrogen formulation or every patient.

### 5.2 Route and formulation

Older regimens containing ethinyl estradiol were associated with substantial thromboembolic risk and are no longer recommended by WPATH for gender-affirming estrogen treatment. SOC8 suggests transdermal estrogen for people at higher VTE risk when estrogen treatment is appropriate. This is a guideline recommendation informed partly by evidence from menopausal hormone therapy and older transgender cohorts; direct randomized comparisons of estrogen routes for major clinical events in transgender populations are lacking.

It is therefore more accurate to say that formulation and route appear to matter than to describe transdermal estrogen as risk-free. Age, smoking, obesity, prior thrombosis, thrombophilia, immobility, surgery, and other cardiovascular factors remain relevant irrespective of route.

### 5.3 Metabolic markers

Changes in lipids, blood pressure, body weight, and insulin sensitivity are generally modest and inconsistent across studies. A 2024 systematic review examining serum estradiol change and cardiovascular risk markers found small associations with body mass index, LDL cholesterol, and systolic blood pressure but emphasized the limited clinical-outcome evidence. Simple claims that estrogen "normalizes" cardiovascular risk toward a healthier female profile are not justified.

## 6. Bone Health

Adult bone outcomes are more reassuring than the inherited report implied. A systematic review and meta-analysis found no significant loss of bone mineral density at major sites in transgender women receiving long-term hormone therapy; lumbar-spine BMD increased slightly in before-and-after studies. The evidence was observational and heterogeneous, but it does not support a general claim that adult feminizing GAHT lowers bone density when adequate sex-steroid exposure is maintained.

Bone risk becomes more important when endogenous sex steroids are suppressed without adequate replacement, after gonadectomy if hormone therapy is discontinued, or when other osteoporosis risk factors are present. The Endocrine Society therefore recommends bone-density testing based on risk rather than a universal schedule for every person receiving GAHT.

Adolescent puberty suppression is different. The 2024 York systematic review found consistent evidence that GnRHa suppress puberty and multiple studies showing reductions in bone-density measures during treatment. Evidence about later recovery after subsequent sex-hormone treatment remains incomplete. This is one reason adolescent bone outcomes should not be inferred from adult GAHT cohorts.

## 7. Mental Health and Quality of Life in Adults

Multiple systematic reviews of adult and mixed-age populations report improvements in depressive symptoms and psychological distress after GAHT. A 2023 systematic review of 46 studies found that hormone therapy was consistently associated with reduced depressive symptoms and psychological distress, while quality-of-life findings were less consistent. The authors also emphasized variable risk of bias, small samples, and inadequate control of confounding.

A separate systematic review found associations with increased quality of life and lower depression and anxiety but concluded that certainty was limited by study design and confounding. More recent prospective adult reviews similarly find a general direction toward improved mental-health outcomes while noting that the available evidence does not isolate hormone effects perfectly from social transition, surgery, psychotherapy, changing support, or regression to the mean.

The evidence therefore supports a cautious statement: many adults experience improved dysphoria-related and psychological outcomes after gender-affirming medical treatment, but the magnitude attributable specifically to hormones is uncertain and cannot be translated into a guaranteed individual mental-health benefit.

Evidence about suicide death is much weaker. Improvements in depression or suicidal ideation should not be presented as demonstrated reductions in suicide mortality unless a study actually measures that endpoint with an appropriate design.

## 8. Cancer and Long-Term Outcomes

Long-term cancer evidence remains limited because modern treatment cohorts are relatively young and treatment regimens have changed over time. Breast tissue develops under estrogen exposure, so breast-cancer risk is not equivalent to that of cisgender men who have not received estrogen. Available cohort and systematic-review evidence suggests risk in transgender women is higher than in cisgender men but lower than in cisgender women, although estimates remain imprecise.

The prostate is generally retained unless removed for another reason. Prostate cancer can therefore still occur even with long-term androgen suppression. The absolute incidence reported in transgender cohorts is low, but low observed incidence should not be interpreted as zero risk or as evidence that routine screening should be ignored. Screening decisions are better based on organ inventory, age, treatment history, family history, and evolving clinical guidance than on gender category alone.

Statements about overall mortality are particularly vulnerable to confounding. Transgender populations differ from comparison populations in socioeconomic exposure, discrimination, mental health, smoking, HIV prevalence, access to care, and treatment history. Cohort mortality differences cannot be attributed directly to GAHT without stronger causal evidence.

## 9. Adolescents: Puberty Suppression and GAHT Require a Separate Evidence Standard

### 9.1 Puberty suppression

GnRH analogues reliably suppress gonadotropin-driven puberty. That pharmacologic effect is well established. The contested question is the balance of downstream benefits and harms when the intervention is used for gender dysphoria or gender incongruence.

A 2024 systematic review of puberty suppression identified 50 studies but found only one high-quality study and 25 moderate-quality studies. It found consistent evidence of puberty suppression and evidence that bone-density accrual and height trajectories may be affected. Evidence for gender dysphoria, psychological health, psychosocial outcomes, cognition, cardiometabolic outcomes, and fertility was limited or inconsistent. The reviewers concluded that no confident conclusions could be drawn for several of those outcomes.

This does not establish that puberty suppression is ineffective or harmful overall. It establishes that the certainty of evidence for many patient-important outcomes is low and that the known physiologic effect of pausing puberty should not be substituted for evidence about long-term psychosocial or health outcomes.

### 9.2 Feminizing or masculinizing hormones in adolescents

The linked 2024 York review of adolescent sex-hormone treatment included 53 studies. It found consistent evidence that treatment induced pubertal secondary sex characteristics. Evidence from mainly pre-post studies suggested improvement in psychological health over approximately 12 months, but evidence for gender dysphoria, body satisfaction, psychosocial and cognitive outcomes, fertility, height, bone health, and cardiometabolic outcomes was limited or inconsistent. Most participants in many studies had also received puberty suppression, limiting attribution to hormones alone.

A separate 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of people younger than 26 reached an even more cautious conclusion: comparative observational evidence for depression, global functioning, gender dysphoria, and several physical outcomes was generally low or very low certainty, leaving considerable uncertainty about both benefit and harm.

The appropriate synthesis is not that adolescent hormones are proven ineffective, nor that their benefits are established to the same certainty as their ability to induce secondary sex characteristics. Short-term mental-health improvement is plausible and supported by observational evidence, while many long-term outcomes remain uncertain.

### 9.3 Claims that should be avoided

The inherited report described early treatment as producing a pelvis and brain that develop along a broadly "female-typical" trajectory and invoked a human developmental "critical period" supported by animal and in-vitro epigenetic findings. Current clinical evidence does not justify those broad claims. Pubertal timing can affect skeletal growth and body composition, but there is insufficient human evidence to describe adolescent GAHT as predictably reorganizing brain development or producing a cisgender-female skeletal outcome.

Similarly, the absence of randomized trials should not automatically be explained as an ethical impossibility. Randomization can be difficult for preference-sensitive and partly irreversible interventions, but stronger prospective comparative designs, standardized outcome measurement, and longer follow-up are still feasible and repeatedly recommended by systematic reviewers.

## 10. Policy and Regulatory Divergence

Medical evidence, clinical recommendations, and legal access have diverged across jurisdictions.

In the United States, WPATH SOC8 and the Endocrine Society guideline recommend pathways under which selected adolescents may receive puberty suppression and later gender-affirming sex hormones after assessment and informed decision-making. At the same time, multiple states have enacted restrictions. In *United States v. Skrmetti* (2025), the U.S. Supreme Court held that Tennessee's restriction on puberty blockers and hormones for minors did not trigger heightened scrutiny under the Equal Protection Clause and survived rational-basis review. That ruling addressed the constitutionality of the Tennessee statute under the claim presented; it did not resolve the medical evidence as a scientific matter.

NHS England has moved in a different clinical-policy direction. Its 2024 policy ended routine commissioning of puberty-suppressing hormones for gender incongruence outside specified research or exceptional pathways. A March 2026 NHS England policy proposition states that feminizing and masculinizing medicines are not available as a routine commissioned treatment for people under 18 with gender incongruence, with transitional provisions for some 16- and 17-year-olds already receiving NHS treatment. NHS England cited insufficient evidence to support routine availability at that time.

These differences are important precisely because they demonstrate that there is no single global regulatory standard. An article should identify the jurisdiction and date of any access claim rather than presenting "the guidelines" or "the law" as universal.

## 11. Off-Label Use and Regulatory Meaning

Many medications used in GAHT are established drugs with FDA-approved indications for other conditions. Using an approved drug for an indication that is not included in its FDA-approved labeling is an off-label use. FDA explains that clinicians may generally prescribe approved drugs off label when they judge doing so medically appropriate, but FDA has not itself determined safety and effectiveness for that unapproved use.

"Off label" should therefore not be used as a synonym for experimental, illegal, or ineffective. Nor should it be presented as equivalent to an FDA finding that a particular gender-affirming indication is safe and effective. It is a regulatory description of the labeled indication.

## 12. What Is Known With Greater and Lesser Certainty

| Domain | More defensible conclusion | Important uncertainty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adult physical feminization | Estrogen with adequate androgen suppression can produce breast development, increased fat mass, reduced lean mass, and reduced androgen-dependent sexual/reproductive function | Magnitude and timing vary; no standard regimen guarantees a particular body shape or breast size |
| Adult fertility | Spermatogenesis can be markedly impaired | Degree, permanence, and recovery after treatment interruption are variable |
| VTE/cardiovascular risk | Observational evidence supports increased VTE risk in transfeminine populations and possible stroke differences versus some comparators | Exact causal contribution of GAHT, route-specific absolute risk, and long-term risk across modern regimens |
| Adult bone health | BMD is generally maintained with adequate sex-steroid exposure | Long-term fracture outcomes and risks in specific subgroups |
| Adult mental health | Most systematic reviews find improvement in depression/distress after GAHT or broader affirmative intervention | Causal magnitude attributable to hormones alone; suicide-mortality effect |
| Puberty suppression | Reliably suppresses pubertal progression | Long-term psychosocial, cognitive, fertility, and bone outcomes remain uncertain; bone accrual can decline during suppression |
| Adolescent GAHT | Reliably induces feminizing/masculinizing secondary sex characteristics; short-term psychological improvement is suggested | Long-term mental health, cognition, fertility, growth, bone, cardiometabolic outcomes; confounding with prior suppression and other care |
| Cancer | Organ-specific risk remains relevant; breast risk is not equivalent to untreated cisgender-male baseline | Precise long-term incidence under modern regimens and optimal screening thresholds |

## 13. Conclusion

Feminizing GAHT has a clear and well-established capacity to alter secondary sex characteristics. For adults, the direction of breast, body-composition, skin, hair, sexual-function, and reproductive changes is substantially better established than the magnitude of long-term cardiovascular, cancer, fertility, or mortality effects. Current evidence supports attention to thromboembolic and cardiovascular risk without treating every estrogen regimen or every patient as having the same risk.

Adolescent treatment requires a different level of evidentiary caution. Puberty suppression reliably pauses pubertal progression, and sex hormones induce secondary sex characteristics, but systematic reviews continue to find low-certainty evidence for many long-term patient-important outcomes. Short-term psychological improvement is supported by observational studies, while important uncertainty remains around bone, growth, fertility, cognition, and longer-term mental health.

The evidence does not justify either a blanket statement that GAHT is uniformly safe and beneficial or a blanket statement that it lacks benefit. A rigorous account separates established physiologic effects from uncertain long-term outcomes, adults from adolescents, puberty suppression from sex-hormone treatment, and empirical evidence from the value judgments embedded in guidelines and public policy.
