# Culture, Compensation, and Unmet Human Needs: Testing a Parasitism Hypothesis

## Abstract

The proposition that culture is “parasitic on unsatisfied human needs” contains several distinct claims that should not be treated as interchangeable. Cultural practices can **respond to deprivation**, cultural goods can **compensate for threatened needs**, cultural systems can **reinforce behaviors that attract attention or social rewards**, and producers can in principle **benefit from the persistence of the deficit that drives repeated use**. Only the last of these establishes parasitism in a strong sense.

Evidence from consumer psychology, social-surrogacy research, compensatory-control theory, and studies of online moral expression supports a substantial but limited conclusion: unmet needs and self-discrepancies can motivate some forms of consumption and belief; favored media can temporarily buffer belonging threats; perceived loss of control can increase support for external systems of order; and social-media feedback can reinforce repeated expressions of outrage. These findings establish real compensatory and reinforcement mechanisms. They do **not**, by themselves, show that culture generally creates, preserves, or strategically refuses to resolve the underlying need.

The universal thesis is also contradicted by evidence that cultural participation can be intrinsically motivated and can contribute to social cohesion, belonging, meaning, and well-being. A more defensible framework therefore distinguishes **compensatory culture**, **extractive culture**, and **generative culture**. “Parasitism” should be reserved for cases where a cultural system not only serves a need but has incentives or mechanisms that systematically benefit from the need remaining unresolved.

## 1. Separating Four Claims

The metaphor of parasitism is analytically useful only if it is made demanding. Four propositions can be separated.

### 1.1 Deficit-responsive culture

A cultural behavior is deficit-responsive when its probability increases after a threat, deprivation, discrepancy, or loss. A lonely person who turns to a familiar television series, a person with reduced perceived control who becomes more attracted to a system promising order, or a consumer who buys a symbolic good after a self-threat all fit this category.

This is an **origin or activation claim**. It says something about what prompts a cultural behavior. It does not say whether the behavior is beneficial or harmful.

### 1.2 Compensatory culture

A cultural behavior is compensatory when it supplies psychological value related to a threatened need. The compensation can be direct, symbolic, substitutive, or temporary.

The consumer-psychology literature provides the clearest formal model. Mandel and colleagues review evidence that self-discrepancies can alter consumption through strategies including direct resolution, symbolic self-completion, dissociation, escapism, and “fluid compensation,” in which success or affirmation in one domain helps respond to threat in another. See [Mandel et al. (2017)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1057740816300304).

Compensation is not inherently pathological. A book may provide perspective during grief; music may regulate emotion; ritual may make uncertainty tolerable; an admired fictional world may restore enough positive affect for a person to re-enter ordinary social life. The same mechanism can also become chronic displacement. Whether compensation helps or harms therefore depends on what follows it.

### 1.3 Reinforcement-dependent culture

A cultural system is reinforcement-dependent when particular expressions or behaviors become more frequent because they receive rewards such as attention, approval, status, money, or algorithmic distribution.

This mechanism is well demonstrated in some online environments. Brady and colleagues analyzed 7,331 Twitter users and 12.7 million tweets and supplemented the observational analyses with preregistered behavioral experiments. Positive social feedback for moral-outrage expression predicted more subsequent outrage expression, while network norms also shaped what users expressed. See [Brady et al. (2021)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8363141/).

Reinforcement can amplify culture without any unmet need at all. People may repeat jokes because they receive laughs or produce technically impressive art because audiences reward it. Reinforcement therefore cannot be equated with parasitism.

### 1.4 Deficit-preserving or extractive culture

The strongest claim requires another step. A cultural institution is **deficit-preserving** when its success depends on an underlying need remaining inadequately resolved and when its incentives, design, or behavior predictably preserve, intensify, or fail to resolve that deficit.

This is the closest analogue to parasitism. Examples would require evidence such as:

- engagement or revenue falling when the underlying deficit is genuinely resolved;
- product design that intentionally or predictably recreates the state that drives use;
- repeated short-term relief paired with long-run persistence or worsening of the originating problem;
- producer optimization around the vulnerable state rather than merely around content preference;
- substitution that crowds out more durable forms of need satisfaction.

These are substantially stronger evidentiary requirements than showing that distressed people consume something or that an industry is profitable.

## 2. What Compensatory-Consumption Research Establishes

The compensatory-consumer-behavior literature directly supports the proposition that some consumption responds to unmet psychological states. Mandel and colleagues organize prior findings around discrepancies between actual and desired selves and show that products can function as psychological responses to threats involving self-esteem, belonging, power, control, and other valued states. See [Mandel et al. (2017)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1057740816300304).

Three implications matter for the cultural hypothesis.

First, **symbolic goods can carry psychological functions beyond their instrumental use**. Clothing, brands, entertainment, collectibles, prestige objects, and other goods can participate in identity regulation.

Second, compensation is heterogeneous. Some behavior directly addresses the discrepancy; some supplies a symbolic substitute; some distracts from the threatened domain. A general theory of “culture feeding on wounds” loses this distinction and therefore loses predictive power.

Third, the existence of compensation does not establish chronic dependence. A compensatory act can help someone recover, can be neutral, or can become a repeated substitute for direct resolution. Longitudinal evidence is needed to distinguish those trajectories.

The most defensible general proposition is therefore not that consumption requires misery. It is that **markets and cultural forms can acquire additional demand because human beings use symbolic objects to regulate discrepancies and threatened needs**.

## 3. Social Surrogacy: A Clear Case of Symbolic Need Satisfaction

Derrick, Gabriel, and Hugenberg's social-surrogacy studies provide a particularly concrete example. Their work found that participants reported turning to favored television programs when lonely and that favored programs could buffer aspects of belonging threat, rejection, negative mood, and self-esteem loss. The effect depended on favored programs rather than television in the abstract, consistent with the idea that familiar mediated relationships can supply a temporary experience of belonging. See [Derrick, Gabriel, and Hugenberg (2009)](https://ubwp.buffalo.edu/gabriellab/wp-content/uploads/sites/65/2025/04/Derrick-J.-L.-Gabriel-S.-Hugenberg-K.-J.-2009.-Social-surrogacy-How-favored-television-programs-provide-the-experience-of-belonging.pdf).

This is strong evidence for **compensatory cultural use**. It does not establish that television causes loneliness, that parasocial bonds generally displace reciprocal relationships, or that producers need viewers to remain lonely.

Those stronger propositions require separate tests. For example, a displacement claim would need to show that surrogate use reduces subsequent attempts at reciprocal connection or that increasing surrogate exposure causes deterioration in direct relationships. A parasitism claim would additionally need to connect that persistence to the incentives or design of the cultural system.

The distinction is important because a surrogate can be a bridge rather than a trap. Temporary symbolic belonging may help regulate a person after rejection without becoming their primary social world. The relevant outcome is not whether a substitute exists but whether it **restores capacity, coexists with direct satisfaction, or progressively replaces it**.

## 4. Compensatory Control: Culture as a Response to Uncertainty

A related mechanism appears in compensatory-control research. Kay and colleagues experimentally manipulated perceived personal control and found that reduced control could increase support for external systems that provide order, including belief in a controlling God and defense of sociopolitical institutions. They also found reciprocal effects in which threats to external systems could increase illusory personal control. See [Kay et al. (2008)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18605849/) and the later review [Kay et al. (2010)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20040614/).

This supports a limited cultural proposition: **belief systems can sometimes function as responses to threatened control and uncertainty**. The evidence is stronger than a purely interpretive claim because the experimental manipulations establish causal effects under the studied conditions.

But again, the evidence does not warrant a universal origin story for religion, ideology, ritual, or institutions. Religious conviction can have many sources and functions; political institutions can provide real coordination rather than symbolic compensation; and cultural systems can endure after the activating threat disappears.

Compensatory-control research therefore supports one mechanism inside culture, not a theory that culture is generated by damage.

## 5. Attention Systems and the Reinforcement of Affective Expression

Digital platforms make the reproduction question especially important because they supply immediate and visible feedback. Likes, reposts, replies, follower growth, and algorithmic distribution can act as social rewards.

Brady and colleagues' work on moral outrage demonstrates that social feedback can affect subsequent expression. In observational and experimental settings, rewarded outrage was more likely to be expressed again, and users also adapted to the expressive norms of their networks. The study does not show that platforms create moral grievances from nothing; it shows that platform-mediated social learning can alter the expression of grievance once it exists. See [Brady et al. (2021)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8363141/).

A separate 2023 study found that observers on social media systematically overestimated how much outrage other users actually felt. That overperception increased beliefs about hostile group norms, polarization, and ideological extremity. See [Brady et al. (2023)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-023-01582-0).

These findings support a theory of **affective amplification**. They justify concern that an attention system can reward expressions that are more emotionally extreme than the underlying population state.

They still do not prove deficit preservation. An outrage-amplifying environment might worsen hostility, might merely alter visible expression, or might sometimes facilitate legitimate collective action. Strong claims about a platform “needing people angry” require evidence about business incentives, design choices, counterfactual algorithms, user outcomes, and the persistence of the underlying grievance.

## 6. Why Market Size Is Not Evidence of Parasitism

The inherited version of this hypothesis leaned heavily on advertising, music, podcast, creator-economy, and other revenue totals. Those figures can establish that cultural markets are economically important. They cannot establish the psychological mechanism under investigation.

Three inferential errors follow from using market size this way.

1. **Scale is not motive.** A large entertainment industry can serve curiosity, pleasure, information, companionship, aesthetics, status, habit, and many other motives simultaneously.
2. **Demand is not dependence.** Repeated consumption does not show that the consumer has an unresolved deficit; people repeatedly consume goods they simply enjoy.
3. **Profit is not deficit preservation.** A seller can profit from satisfying a need. Parasitism requires evidence that persistence of the unmet need is beneficial to the system in a way that shapes behavior or design.

Industry-revenue figures are therefore excluded from the evidentiary core. The appropriate evidence is psychological, longitudinal, experimental, behavioral, and institutional.

## 7. Culture Can Also Be Generative

A universal parasitism theory fails because cultural activity often occurs under conditions of need satisfaction and can itself create durable goods rather than merely symbolic substitutes.

Self-determination theory is one countermodel. It treats intrinsic motivation as an expression of human activity that flourishes when autonomy and competence are supported; relatedness also supports internalization and functioning in social settings. People play, explore, learn, and create because activities are interesting and enjoyable, not only because a deficit demands compensation. See [Ryan and Deci (2000)](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_SDT.pdf).

Arts research supplies another counterexample. A 2025 integrative review of community arts participation, social cohesion, and well-being synthesized 18 eligible studies from 12 countries. It found evidence consistent with arts participation supporting relationships, belonging, solidarity, and well-being, while emphasizing that prospective research is still needed to establish mechanisms more firmly. See [Sonke et al. (2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12098425/).

A separate systematic review of arts and creativity among adults aged 50 and older found promising associations between music, singing, visual arts, social connectedness, affective states, and well-being, while the strength of evidence varied by art form and outcome. See [Curtis et al. (2022)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9770187/).

These findings do not imply that culture is inherently healing. They establish something narrower and sufficient to defeat the universal thesis: **cultural participation can be constitutive of relationship, competence, meaning, and flourishing rather than merely a substitute for their absence**.

## 8. A Three-Type Framework

The evidence is better organized by distinguishing three ideal types.

| Type | Relationship to human needs | Diagnostic evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Compensatory culture** | Responds to a threatened or unsatisfied need and provides partial or symbolic relief | need threat predicts use; use reduces distress or discrepancy at least temporarily |
| **Extractive culture** | Benefits from repeated engagement generated by a deficit and has mechanisms that preserve, recreate, intensify, or exploit that deficit | persistent deficit predicts engagement; design/reward structure sustains the state; durable resolution reduces demand; crowd-out or worsening is observable |
| **Generative culture** | Creates or deepens goods such as skill, meaning, belonging, knowledge, aesthetic experience, or collective capacity | participation increases durable capability, relationship, understanding, or well-being; activity can be intrinsically motivated |

These are analytical types rather than mutually exclusive industries. The same platform, genre, game, ritual, fandom, or art practice can contain all three mechanisms.

A multiplayer game can provide real friendship and skill development, compensate temporarily for loneliness, and simultaneously use engagement mechanics designed to maximize repeated play. A fandom can create reciprocal communities while also generating parasocial substitutes. Political media can inform citizens while rewarding outrage. A prestigious academic practice can create knowledge while also turning scarcity and status competition into motivational resources.

The framework is therefore **mechanism-specific rather than object-specific**. Calling an entire medium parasitic in advance would merely restate the conclusion that empirical work is supposed to test.

## 9. How the Strong Hypothesis Could Be Tested

The strong hypothesis predicts a feedback loop:

**need frustration → cultural engagement → short-term relief/reward → unresolved or intensified need → repeated engagement**.

A credible empirical program would need to test each arrow rather than infer the loop from cross-sectional correlations.

### 9.1 Individual-level longitudinal design

A panel study could repeatedly measure:

- loneliness, belonging, perceived control, self-discrepancy, status threat, or another specified deficit;
- exposure to particular cultural products or platform behaviors;
- immediate affective or motivational effects;
- direct attempts to satisfy the underlying need outside the cultural surrogate;
- change in the deficit over weeks or months;
- subsequent engagement.

The crucial discriminator is the long-run path. If use supplies temporary relief while the underlying need remains unchanged, that supports compensation. If use predicts worsening or displacement of direct satisfaction, a stronger extractive interpretation becomes plausible. If use predicts improvement in relationships or capability, the same practice is functioning generatively.

### 9.2 Platform and institutional design

System-level evidence would require analysis of incentives and counterfactual designs. Useful tests include:

- whether engagement-maximizing ranking disproportionately distributes content associated with threat or insecurity;
- whether reducing such amplification changes user well-being or merely changes expression;
- whether firms identify vulnerable states as segmentation or retention variables;
- whether product changes that improve durable need satisfaction lower retention and are consequently disfavored;
- whether alternative designs can preserve user value without recreating the vulnerable state.

The distinction between unintended reinforcement and strategic exploitation is essential. A platform can amplify outrage through ordinary feedback dynamics without executives deliberately deciding to make users angry.

### 9.3 Sector comparison

The theory also predicts variation across cultural sectors. Practices based on co-creation, reciprocal participation, skill accumulation, or durable community should be less deficit-dependent than products built around repeated short episodes of relief and highly optimized retention. That prediction can be compared across communities, games, social platforms, religious organizations, entertainment genres, and consumer categories.

## 10. Background Need Prevalence Does Not Establish Causation

Unmet social needs are widespread enough to make compensatory mechanisms socially important. The WHO Commission on Social Connection estimated that about 16% of people globally reported loneliness during 2014–2023, with higher estimated prevalence among adolescents and young adults. See [WHO (2025)](https://iris.who.int/bitstream/handle/10665/381746/9789240112360-eng.pdf?sequence=1).

That prevalence is context, not proof. A society can have widespread loneliness and a large entertainment sector without the first causing the second. Establishing the cultural hypothesis requires within-person, between-person, or institutional evidence connecting a specified deficit to a specified behavior and then tracing outcomes over time.

This point protects the theory from becoming unfalsifiable. If every cultural behavior observed in a distressed society is interpreted as compensation, then no possible observation could count against the theory.

## 11. Judgment on the Hypothesis

The evidence supports the hypothesis at three different strengths.

### Supported: some culture is deficit-responsive and compensatory

Consumer-behavior, social-surrogacy, and compensatory-control research establish that threatened needs and discrepancies can cause or predict symbolic and cultural responses. This is not merely metaphorical.

### Supported in specified environments: cultural expression can be socially reinforced

Online outrage research shows that visible social feedback and local expressive norms can increase subsequent outrage expression. Cultural reproduction is therefore partly shaped by reward structures rather than only by prior private preference.

### Not established as a general proposition: culture depends on preserving unmet needs

The evidence reviewed here does not show that culture as a whole, or even most commercial culture, systematically requires the underlying human deficit to remain unresolved. Market size and repeat use are insufficient. Direct evidence of preservation, worsening, displacement, or incentive-driven exploitation is required.

The strong thesis should therefore be retained as a **testable hypothesis about particular systems**, not as a settled theory of culture.

## Conclusion

Human need and cultural production are deeply entangled, but the relation is not adequately described by a single parasite metaphor.

People sometimes use cultural goods as substitutes when direct satisfaction is unavailable. They sometimes use symbols and institutions to recover control, belonging, or self-worth. Platforms can reinforce emotionally charged expression through visible rewards and network norms. Those mechanisms create genuine possibilities for exploitation.

Culture can also satisfy needs directly, build relationships, increase competence, create knowledge, coordinate communities, and provide intrinsically motivated activity. A song sung with others can be a product, a surrogate, and a real social bond at the same time.

The analytically useful boundary is therefore not **culture versus need satisfaction**. It is **compensation versus extraction versus generation**. A cultural system deserves the stronger label “parasitic” only when evidence shows that it benefits from a deficit in a way that helps keep the deficit alive. That is a much harder claim than showing that wounded people consume culture—and precisely because it is harder, it is capable of being tested.
