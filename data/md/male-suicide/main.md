# Male Suicide in the United States Since 2000

## Abstract

Suicide mortality in the United States is strongly concentrated among males. Final National Vital Statistics System data show that 39,046 males died by suicide in 2023, representing nearly four-fifths of U.S. suicide deaths that year. The male age-adjusted suicide rate was 22.7 deaths per 100,000 in 2023, compared with 5.9 among females. The male rate rose substantially from the mid-2000s through 2018, but it did not show a statistically significant trend from 2018 through 2023. Final all-sex mortality data for 2024 show a modest national decline in suicide mortality, although a comparable final sex-specific 2024 trend analysis is not yet available in the cited National Center for Health Statistics reports.

The male mortality burden is heterogeneous. In 2023, rates were highest among males age 75 and older, while males ages 25–44 and 45–64 also had high rates. Firearms are the leading means of suicide among males. Earlier NCHS analyses also show large racial and ethnic disparities, particularly high mortality among American Indian and Alaska Native males. Veterans remain an important high-risk population: the Department of Veterans Affairs reported 6,398 Veteran suicide deaths in 2023 and a male Veteran suicide rate of 37.8 per 100,000.

Mortality should be distinguished from self-reported suicidal thoughts and attempts, and risk or protective domains should not be treated as deterministic causes. The prevention evidence is strongest when intervention claims are reported at the level actually measured, including suicide-specific psychotherapy, collaborative safety planning and follow-up, lethal-means safety, continuity of care, treatment of underlying psychiatric and substance-use disorders, and broader public-health strategies.

## 1. Scope and Evidence Standard

The subject is male suicide in the United States from the early 2000s through the most recent authoritative data available at revision time. The central outcome is death by suicide. Suicidal thoughts, plans, and nonfatal attempts are treated separately because they are measured through surveys and clinical records rather than death certificates and because their sex distribution differs from suicide mortality.

The epidemiological backbone is the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) and the National Vital Statistics System (NVSS). NCHS Data Brief 541 provides final sex-specific trend data through 2023, while Data Brief 548 provides final all-sex mortality through 2024. Additional population evidence comes from the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration's 2024 National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH), the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA), and CDC suicide-prevention publications. Intervention claims rely primarily on randomized trials, large clinical evaluations, systematic reviews, and federal evidence syntheses.

Several distinctions are important throughout:

1. **Counts, crude rates, and age-adjusted rates are not interchangeable.** Age-adjusted rates are useful for comparing populations and periods with different age structures; age-specific rates describe actual risk within an age group.
2. **Association is not causation.** Economic hardship, relationship disruption, psychiatric illness, substance use, firearm access, social isolation, occupation, veteran status, and other variables can be associated with suicide risk without functioning as simple independent causes.
3. **Population patterns do not diagnose individuals.** Most people with any single risk factor do not die by suicide, and some people who die by suicide have no documented psychiatric diagnosis or prior attempt.
4. **Sex-specific mortality data should not be inferred from all-sex updates.** The final 2024 overall suicide decline is relevant, but it does not by itself establish the direction or magnitude of a male-specific 2024 change.
5. **Intervention evidence is graded by design and endpoint.** Reductions in suicidal behavior, attempts, or ideation are important but are not automatically equivalent to demonstrated reductions in suicide deaths.

## 2. National Mortality Trend

NCHS final data show a long rise in male suicide mortality followed by a recent plateau. The male age-adjusted rate was 18.1 per 100,000 in 2003 and increased to 22.8 in 2018. NCHS joinpoint analysis found a significant increase from 2003 through 2018, but no statistically significant trend from 2018 through 2023. The rate was 23.0 in 2022 and declined significantly to 22.7 in 2023. In 2023, 39,046 males died by suicide, compared with 10,270 females; the male rate was roughly four times the female rate.

| Year | Male suicide deaths | Male age-adjusted rate per 100,000 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 2003 | 25,203 | 18.1 |
| 2010 | 30,277 | 19.8 |
| 2015 | 33,994 | 21.1 |
| 2018 | 37,761 | 22.8 |
| 2020 | 36,551 | 22.0 |
| 2022 | 39,273 | 23.0 |
| 2023 | 39,046 | 22.7 |

Source: NCHS, *Changes in Suicide Rates in the United States From 2022 to 2023* (Data Brief 541).

The recent pattern matters for interpretation. A continuing linear increase should not be inferred from the post-2020 rebound. The statistically supported description is more restrained: male suicide mortality rose substantially through 2018 and remained at a high level without a significant trend from 2018 through 2023.

Final 2024 mortality data add another piece of context. NCHS reported 48,824 suicide deaths across both sexes in 2024 and an overall age-adjusted rate of 13.7 per 100,000, down 2.8% from 14.1 in 2023. Suicide was the tenth leading cause of death in 2024. That is evidence of a national decline, but the cited 2024 report does not provide the same male-specific trend table as Data Brief 541. A male-specific 2024 rate should therefore be taken from a dedicated NCHS or CDC analysis when one becomes available rather than inferred from the all-sex change.

The male-to-female mortality difference is persistent, but it should not be interpreted as evidence that men experience suicidal distress four times as often. Survey measures of ideation and attempts show a different pattern, discussed below. Mortality reflects not only the prevalence of suicidal crises but also method lethality, timing, access to care, age distribution, and other factors.

## 3. Age Pattern

Male suicide mortality varies sharply by age. In 2023, NCHS reported the following age-specific rates:

| Male age group | Suicide deaths, 2023 | Rate per 100,000 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 10–14 | 268 | 2.5 |
| 15–24 | 4,760 | 21.2 |
| 25–44 | 13,543 | 29.8 |
| 45–64 | 11,888 | 29.2 |
| 65–74 | 4,330 | 26.5 |
| 75+ | 4,252 | 40.7 |

The oldest male group had the highest rate in both 2022 and 2023. Its rate decreased significantly from 43.9 to 40.7 between those years. NCHS found no statistically significant 2022–2023 change in the other male age groups.

This pattern complicates simple descriptions of a single "middle-aged male suicide crisis." Males ages 25–64 account for a large absolute number of deaths because they combine high rates with large populations. Males age 75 and older have the highest age-specific rate. Younger males have lower mortality rates than those adult groups but still experience a substantial burden, and suicide ranks highly among causes of death at younger ages because mortality from many other causes is uncommon.

Long-run trajectories also differ by age. NCHS found that male rates rose across age groups over the 2001–2021 period, but later changes were not uniform. For example, the 45–64 rate declined from 2018 through 2021, while several other male age groups increased from 2020 to 2021. The 2002–2022 analysis likewise found that male rates at ages 10–14 and 15–24 declined from 2020 through 2022. Claims about "young men," "middle-aged men," or "older men" should therefore specify the period and age band rather than generalizing from a single national trend.

## 4. Means of Suicide and Lethality

Means matter because methods differ substantially in case fatality and because access can affect whether an acute suicidal crisis becomes fatal. Firearms are the leading means of suicide among U.S. males.

NCHS reported the following age-adjusted male suicide rates by means in 2022:

| Means | Rate per 100,000 males |
| --- | ---: |
| Firearm | 13.5 |
| Suffocation | 5.8 |
| Poisoning | 1.8 |
| Other means | 1.8 |

The male firearm-suicide rate increased from 10.3 per 100,000 in 2006 to 13.5 in 2022. Firearms remained the leading male method throughout the 2002–2022 period. CDC's broader 2023 suicide data likewise show firearms accounting for more than half of U.S. suicide deaths across sexes.

The epidemiological importance of firearms does not imply that firearm access is the sole or sufficient explanation for male suicide. It does mean that lethal-means safety is a central prevention domain. Suicidal crises can fluctuate over short periods, and reducing immediate access to highly lethal means can create time for the crisis to pass or for intervention to occur. Federal suicide-prevention guidance therefore includes reducing access to lethal means among people at risk as part of a broader prevention strategy.

A careful account should distinguish several propositions that are sometimes collapsed together:

- psychiatric or social distress can generate suicidal thoughts without any firearm involvement;
- firearm access can increase the probability that an attempt is fatal;
- safe-storage and temporary-separation practices are directed at periods of elevated risk rather than at explaining why the underlying distress arose;
- population firearm policy, household storage practices, and clinician-family lethal-means counseling are distinct interventions with different evidence bases and legal contexts.

## 5. Race and Ethnicity

The most recent NCHS report cited here with detailed male rates by race and Hispanic origin is based on final 2021 mortality data. In that analysis, age-adjusted male suicide rates were:

| Group | Male rate per 100,000, 2021 |
| --- | ---: |
| American Indian or Alaska Native, non-Hispanic | 42.6 |
| White, non-Hispanic | 28.0 |
| Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, non-Hispanic | 19.5 |
| Black, non-Hispanic | 14.6 |
| Hispanic | 12.8 |
| Asian, non-Hispanic | 9.9 |

American Indian or Alaska Native males had the highest rate among the groups compared, while Asian males had the lowest. From 2020 to 2021, rates increased significantly for American Indian or Alaska Native, Black, and White males; changes for Hispanic, Asian, and Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander males were not statistically significant.

These estimates require an important measurement caveat. NCHS notes that race and Hispanic-origin misclassification on death certificates can underestimate mortality rates, with particularly large underestimation possible for American Indian or Alaska Native populations. The 2021 values therefore should not be treated as perfect measurements of group differences.

Racial categories also aggregate populations with different ages, regions, socioeconomic conditions, cultural contexts, health-care access, and firearm exposure. The appropriate conclusion is not that race itself is a simple causal risk factor. The data identify unequal burdens that require more specific analysis of the mechanisms and environments producing them.

## 6. Occupation, Economic Conditions, and Social Context

Suicide mortality also varies across occupational settings. CDC analysis of the civilian noninstitutionalized working population in 2021 found an overall occupational suicide rate of 32.0 per 100,000 among males. Rates were particularly high in several male-dominated industries, including mining, construction, agriculture/forestry/fishing/hunting, and some repair and service sectors. The male construction-industry rate was 56.0 per 100,000; the male construction-and-extraction occupation rate was 65.6.

Occupational differences can reflect multiple overlapping exposures: job insecurity, economic volatility, physical injury and pain, substance use, sleep disruption, workplace culture, access to lethal means, demographic composition, and geographic concentration. They do not demonstrate that the occupation itself causes suicide. They do identify workplaces as potentially important settings for prevention, including peer norms, supervisor training, access to care, and post-crisis follow-up.

Economic distress has repeatedly been associated with suicide at both individual and population levels, but causal attribution is difficult. Job loss can co-occur with debt, health problems, relationship strain, substance use, loss of social role, and reduced insurance coverage. Economic expansions and recessions also affect populations unevenly. The CDC's current prevention framework includes strengthening household financial security and stabilizing housing among its evidence-informed population strategies, reflecting the relevance of material conditions without reducing suicide to a single economic variable.

Relationship disruption and social isolation are likewise meaningful risk domains. Marriage, separation, divorce, bereavement, living arrangement, parenthood, friendship networks, and community participation are correlated in complex ways with age, health, income, and gender norms. Interpersonal loss and low connectedness are common circumstances in suicide research, and healthy social connection is a recognized protective domain in federal prevention frameworks. Strong numerical claims about divorce effects require population-, period-, and adjustment-specific primary evidence rather than popular-press summaries.

## 7. Veterans

Veteran suicide deserves separate treatment because Veteran populations differ from the general population in age, sex composition, military exposures, health conditions, and access to a dedicated health system.

The VA's 2025 National Veteran Suicide Prevention Annual Report, released with data through 2023, reported:

- 6,398 Veteran suicide deaths in 2023, 44 fewer than in 2022;
- an overall Veteran suicide rate of 35.2 per 100,000;
- a male Veteran suicide rate of 37.8 per 100,000, up from 37.3 in 2022;
- a female Veteran rate of 13.9, up from 13.7;
- 39% of Veterans who died by suicide in 2023 had received Veterans Health Administration care in 2022 or 2023, while 61% had not.

The report also emphasizes the importance of firearms in Veteran suicide. In 2023, 73.3% of Veteran suicide deaths involved firearms, compared with 52.9% among non-Veteran U.S. adults. That difference makes lethal-means safety especially salient in Veteran prevention.

The VA's in-depth case review illustrates why single-factor explanations are inadequate. Commonly documented concerns included pain, sleep problems, health problems, physical decline, relationship problems, hopelessness, impulsivity or poor control, unsecured firearms, and financial loss. These circumstances overlap rather than forming mutually exclusive categories.

Comparisons between Veterans and non-Veterans should account for differences in age and sex distribution. Raw rates alone should not be described as a clean causal effect of military service. The VA publishes age- and sex-adjusted analyses for that reason.

## 8. Suicidal Thoughts and Attempts

Mortality data capture deaths; they do not measure the full population experiencing suicidal distress. NSDUH provides complementary self-report estimates for the civilian, noninstitutionalized population.

In 2024, an estimated 14.3 million U.S. adults reported serious thoughts of suicide in the past year. The estimated prevalence was 5.5% among adult males and 5.5% among adult females. Approximately 7.0 million males and 7.4 million females were estimated to have had serious thoughts.

NSDUH estimated about 2.2 million adult suicide attempts in 2024, including approximately 1.0 million among males and 1.2 million among females. These survey estimates are subject to sampling error, nonresponse, question interpretation, and exclusion of institutionalized populations, but they demonstrate a crucial point: the several-fold male excess in suicide deaths is not mirrored by an equally large male excess in self-reported suicidal thoughts.

The divergence between thoughts, nonfatal attempts, and deaths is one reason "men die by suicide more often because men are more suicidal" is too simple. Differences in method lethality, age, planning, access to means, treatment engagement, rescue probability, and other factors can influence the transition from distress to fatal outcome.

For adolescents ages 12–17, NSDUH's 2024 survey found serious suicidal thoughts reported by 6.3% of males and 14.1% of females. An estimated 1.7% of male adolescents and 3.8% of female adolescents reported a suicide attempt. These estimates reinforce the need to distinguish nonfatal suicidal behavior from mortality rather than applying the adult male death ratio to youth distress.

## 9. Risk and Protective Domains

No checklist can accurately predict suicide at the individual level. Risk is dynamic and usually emerges from combinations of vulnerabilities, acute stressors, access to means, and protective resources. The following domains are repeatedly represented in surveillance, clinical research, and federal prevention frameworks.

### 9.1 Mental health and substance use

Mood disorders, substance-use disorders, trauma-related disorders, psychosis, and other psychiatric conditions are associated with elevated suicide risk. Yet documented diagnosis is neither necessary nor sufficient for suicide. Some people who die by suicide have no known diagnosis, while most people with psychiatric disorders do not die by suicide.

This distinction matters for prevention. Treating depression, bipolar disorder, psychosis, post-traumatic stress, alcohol-use disorder, opioid-use disorder, and other conditions according to evidence-based clinical standards is important, but suicide-specific assessment and care may still be needed. Conversely, suicide prevention cannot be reduced to psychiatric treatment because material, interpersonal, medical, and environmental circumstances also matter.

Substance use can increase risk through chronic disorder, intoxication, disinhibition, worsening mood, interpersonal disruption, and interaction with other stressors. Treatment of substance-use disorders is therefore both a clinical objective in its own right and part of a broader suicide-prevention system.

### 9.2 Physical illness, pain, and sleep

Chronic pain, disabling illness, sleep disturbance, and declining physical function are recurrent concerns in suicide research, particularly in older adults and Veterans. These factors can interact with depression, opioid exposure, loss of independence, financial strain, and perceived burden. Their importance argues for integrated medical and behavioral care rather than treating suicide risk as separate from physical health.

### 9.3 Relationship disruption and connectedness

Intimate-partner problems, bereavement, family conflict, and low social connectedness appear frequently in violent-death surveillance and clinical histories. Healthy connection can provide emotional support, practical assistance, monitoring during crises, and reasons for continued engagement in life. CDC therefore includes promotion of healthy peer norms and shared community activity within its prevention framework.

"Marriage protects men" should not be turned into a universal prescription. Marital status is an imperfect proxy for relationship quality and social connection, and harmful relationships can themselves be sources of distress. The more general protective construct is reliable, supportive connection.

### 9.4 Economic and occupational stress

Unemployment, financial loss, housing instability, and occupational disruption can contribute to acute crises and chronic distress. Effects may be particularly consequential when employment is tied to identity, health insurance, social network, or family obligations. Population interventions that reduce financial and housing instability are consequently part of CDC's prevention strategy.

### 9.5 Gender norms and help-seeking

Some norms culturally associated with masculinity—especially rigid self-reliance, emotional concealment, and reluctance to seek help—can impede disclosure and treatment engagement. These associations should not be turned into a claim that masculinity as a whole causes suicide. Male populations are heterogeneous, and traits such as responsibility, group loyalty, problem-solving, or commitment to others can also be mobilized protectively.

The practical issue is accessibility: prevention systems must be usable by people who do not spontaneously identify themselves as psychiatric patients. Primary care, workplaces, veteran organizations, peer networks, remote services, and low-friction follow-up can reach some men who would not enter specialty mental-health care on their own.

### 9.6 Lethal means

Access to highly lethal means is one of the most actionable environmental components of suicide prevention. Firearms are particularly important for U.S. males because of their dominant role in male suicide deaths. Lethal-means safety can include secure storage, voluntary temporary separation from firearms or other lethal means during a crisis, and counseling that involves the patient and support system where appropriate.

The goal is not to claim that means restriction resolves underlying suffering. It reduces the probability that a transient or escalating crisis ends in an irreversible death.

## 10. What the Intervention Evidence Supports

A useful evidence hierarchy distinguishes suicide-specific interventions from general treatment and randomized evidence from observational program evaluations.

### 10.1 Suicide-focused cognitive therapy

A randomized controlled trial of 120 adults evaluated after a suicide attempt compared a ten-session cognitive therapy intervention with enhanced usual care. Over 18 months, 24.1% of the cognitive-therapy group and 41.6% of the usual-care group made at least one repeat attempt. The hazard of reattempt was about half in the intervention group (hazard ratio 0.51, 95% CI 0.26–0.997).

This trial supports structured psychotherapy directed specifically at suicidal behavior. It was not a male-specific trial—about 39% of participants were men—so it should not be presented as proof of a uniquely male treatment effect.

### 10.2 Safety planning with follow-up

A large Veterans Health Administration emergency-department evaluation compared the Safety Planning Intervention plus structured follow-up with usual care. Approximately 88% of participants were men. During six months of follow-up, suicidal behavior was recorded in 3.03% of the intervention group and 5.29% of the comparison group. The intervention was associated with 45% fewer suicidal behaviors and more than twice the odds of attending an outpatient mental-health visit.

The study was a multi-site cohort comparison rather than a randomized trial, so residual differences between groups cannot be excluded. Its size, male-heavy Veteran sample, practical design, and consistency with broader continuity-of-care principles nevertheless make it directly relevant to male suicide prevention.

Safety planning is not a "no-suicide contract." It is a collaborative, concrete plan for recognizing warning signs, using internal coping strategies, contacting supportive people and services, making the environment safer, and obtaining professional help. The 2024 National Strategy for Suicide Prevention incorporates collaborative safety planning, lethal-means safety counseling, suicide-specific treatment, peer support where available, and follow-up after discharge into its clinical pathway.

### 10.3 Continuity of care and post-crisis follow-up

Risk can remain elevated after emergency treatment, psychiatric discharge, or a nonfatal attempt. Systems that lose contact with patients during transitions create avoidable gaps. Federal guidance therefore emphasizes prompt follow-up, reassessment, review of the safety plan, support for missed appointments, and connection back to ongoing care.

For men who are reluctant to seek specialty care, this system-level responsibility is especially important. Prevention should not depend entirely on the person in crisis initiating every subsequent contact.

### 10.4 Treatment of underlying psychiatric disorders

Treatment of depression, bipolar disorder, psychotic disorders, post-traumatic stress, and substance-use disorders is an essential component of care when those conditions are present. Drug-specific claims require particular caution.

Lithium is a useful example of why caution is necessary. Older literature and observational studies have often suggested an antisuicidal benefit in mood disorders. Contemporary meta-analyses restricted to randomized trials found lower point estimates for suicide among lithium-treated patients but wide confidence intervals crossing the null; another 2022 randomized-trial review found no statistically significant reduction in suicide or nonfatal suicidal behavior. The evidence therefore does not support a universal statement that lithium reduces suicide by a fixed percentage. Decisions about lithium belong to condition-specific psychiatric treatment, where benefits, toxicity, monitoring, and alternatives can be considered.

Rapid-acting treatments such as ketamine or esketamine can reduce depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation in selected clinical contexts, but changes in ideation are not equivalent to demonstrated reductions in suicide mortality. They should not be advertised in a population report as stand-alone suicide-prevention solutions.

### 10.5 Lethal-means safety

CDC's Suicide Prevention Resource for Action places reduction of access to lethal means among people at risk within its "create protective environments" strategy. The 2024 National Strategy likewise includes lethal-means safety counseling in clinical care pathways.

This evidence domain includes interventions at different levels: household storage practices, temporary voluntary separation during periods of risk, clinical counseling, environmental barriers at high-risk locations, and laws or policies affecting access. Effects are context-specific, and policy evaluation can be confounded by other state or population differences. The strongest general conclusion is that reducing immediate access to highly lethal means during periods of elevated risk is an evidence-supported component of comprehensive prevention.

### 10.6 Crisis systems and 988

The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is a national access point to a network of crisis centers. Its rapid growth in contact volume demonstrates use and reach, but contact volume should not be equated with proof that 988 has caused a particular change in national suicide mortality. Mortality effects are difficult to isolate from concurrent changes in health care, substance use, firearms, economic conditions, and other prevention programs.

For research purposes, 988 is best described as crisis-system infrastructure whose performance can be evaluated through response times, answer rates, referral patterns, repeat contacts, emergency-service involvement, and longer-term outcomes rather than through unsupported attribution of national mortality trends.

## 11. Public-Health Strategy

The current federal approach is explicitly multi-level. CDC's Suicide Prevention Resource for Action organizes prevention into seven broad strategies:

1. strengthen economic supports;
2. create protective environments;
3. improve access and delivery of suicide care;
4. promote healthy connections;
5. teach coping and problem-solving skills;
6. identify and support people at risk;
7. lessen harms and prevent future risk.

This framework is useful because it avoids the false choice between "mental-health treatment" and "social causes." Suicide prevention can simultaneously involve clinical care, substance-use treatment, economic stability, social connection, workplace practices, safe environments, post-crisis follow-up, and responsible communication.

For male populations, several applications are particularly salient:

- **Primary and general medical care:** Men who do not seek specialty mental-health services may still have contact with primary care, emergency departments, pain care, or substance-use treatment.
- **Workplaces:** High rates in construction, mining, agriculture, and related occupations make workplace culture, peer recognition, leave policies, and access to care relevant.
- **Veteran systems and community organizations:** Many Veterans who die by suicide are not recent VHA users, requiring prevention beyond VA clinical settings.
- **Older-adult care:** The high rate among men age 75 and older requires attention to health decline, pain, bereavement, isolation, and firearm access without assuming that late-life suicide is an inevitable consequence of aging.
- **Youth and young-adult systems:** Mortality is lower than in older male groups, but suicidal thoughts and attempts are common enough to warrant school, family, health-care, and community prevention.

The evidence does not support a single "male suicide program" that can substitute for these systems. Sex can help identify a large mortality burden, but effective intervention still depends on age, setting, clinical condition, social circumstances, and means access.

## 12. Forecasting Limits

A simple straight-line extrapolation of suicide rates is not an adequate basis for a 2030 point forecast. NCHS trend analysis finds no significant change in the male age-adjusted rate from 2018 through 2023, so fitting one slope across an earlier rising period can misrepresent the current trajectory.

An intervention scenario also cannot be converted into an estimate of "lives saved" unless the modeled rate reduction is tied to a defined intervention or intervention package with a defensible causal effect. An assumed target is not an estimated treatment effect.

Suicide mortality can be affected by economic shocks, demographic change, substance-use patterns, firearm exposure, health-system changes, coding practices, and prevention programs. Credible forecasting would require explicit uncertainty intervals, model validation, sensitivity analysis, and justified structural assumptions. Scenario modeling can be useful for planning when presented as scenario analysis rather than prediction.

## 13. Data and Interpretation Limits

### 13.1 Mortality classification

Suicide is determined from death-certificate and death-investigation information. Intent can be difficult to establish in some poisoning, single-vehicle, drowning, or other ambiguous deaths. Misclassification can therefore affect counts. The magnitude and direction of misclassification are not uniform across methods or populations.

### 13.2 Race and ethnicity

Death-certificate race and Hispanic-origin classification can be inaccurate. NCHS warns that underestimation is particularly substantial for American Indian or Alaska Native populations. Group comparisons should therefore include uncertainty about classification as well as differences in age structure, geography, and other exposures.

### 13.3 Sexual orientation and gender identity

National death certificates do not provide a consistent, complete measure of sexual orientation or gender identity. Surveys show elevated suicidal thoughts and attempts in some sexual- and gender-minority populations, especially youth, but these data should not be converted into precise national male suicide-death rates. A rigorous male mortality report should acknowledge this measurement gap rather than invent a death-rate estimate from survey attempts.

### 13.4 Survey estimates

NSDUH measures self-reported thoughts, plans, and attempts among the civilian noninstitutionalized population. Results can be affected by nonresponse, stigma, question interpretation, sampling variation, and methodological changes. Survey prevalence should not be added mechanically to mortality counts or treated as a clinical diagnosis.

### 13.5 Documented diagnosis

Clinical and surveillance records undercount conditions that were never diagnosed or disclosed. Conversely, the presence of a diagnosis does not establish that it caused a death. Psychological-autopsy estimates, health-record diagnoses, and family reports measure different constructs.

### 13.6 Intervention endpoints

Many prevention studies are powered to detect attempts, repeat self-harm, treatment engagement, or ideation rather than suicide death. These outcomes matter, but evidence should be described at the endpoint actually measured.

### 13.7 Rapidly changing data

National mortality data are revised on a schedule, and current-year provisional estimates can differ from final data. Final NCHS reports should be preferred where available, and each subgroup estimate should be labeled with its data year. Older subgroup tables should be replaced only when equivalent authoritative data are available rather than mixing final and provisional series without warning.

## 14. Conclusion

Male suicide remains a major U.S. public-health burden. The best current sex-specific NCHS evidence shows a substantial rise in male suicide mortality through 2018 followed by a high plateau through 2023, not a continuously accelerating linear trend. In 2023, 39,046 males died by suicide and the male age-adjusted rate was 22.7 per 100,000. Men age 75 and older had the highest age-specific rate, while men ages 25–64 accounted for a large share of deaths. Firearms were the leading means of male suicide, and large disparities persisted across racial, occupational, and Veteran populations.

At the same time, survey evidence shows that the male mortality excess cannot be understood simply as a fourfold excess of suicidal thoughts. In 2024 NSDUH data, adult males and females reported serious suicidal thoughts at the same estimated prevalence, while suicide deaths remained overwhelmingly male. Any explanation must therefore consider the transition from distress to fatal behavior, including method lethality, age, health, social context, treatment engagement, and access to means.

The strongest prevention model is correspondingly plural. Suicide-specific psychotherapy, collaborative safety planning, structured follow-up, lethal-means safety, treatment of underlying psychiatric and substance-use disorders, crisis access, social connection, economic support, and safer systems of care address different parts of the risk pathway. None should be presented as a universal cure, and medication-specific claims require particular caution.

The appropriate editorial standard for this subject is therefore conservative: use final surveillance data when available, separate associations from causal claims, distinguish mortality from ideation and attempts, report intervention outcomes at the level actually measured, and remove forecasts or prescriptions that exceed the evidence.
