# Before “Scientific Socialism”: Marx, Engels, and the Communist Manifesto

## Executive finding

The *Manifesto of the Communist Party* is often described retrospectively as a foundational statement of **scientific socialism**. That description is defensible only if the chronology is kept straight.

Marx and Engels did **not** present the 1848 pamphlet under the name “scientific socialism,” and the phrase does not appear in the *Manifesto*. What the text does contain is a cluster of arguments that Engels later treated as the theoretical basis of scientific socialism: social orders are explained through historically changing relations of production and class conflict; communism is presented not as an ideal blueprint invented by a reformer but as a movement arising from existing social relations; and rival socialist schools are criticized when they stand outside, soften, or attempt to bypass the developing class struggle.

The explicit contrast between **utopian** and **scientific** socialism belongs to a later stage of Marx and Engels’s intellectual and political vocabulary. It was codified most famously in Engels’s *Anti-Dühring* (1876–78) and in the 1880 French pamphlet *Socialisme utopique et Socialisme scientifique*, later translated into English as *Socialism: Utopian and Scientific*.

The historically accurate formulation is therefore:

> **The Communist Manifesto did not coin or announce “scientific socialism.” It articulated central historical and political claims that Engels later organized and popularized under that label.**

That distinction matters because otherwise three different questions collapse into one: why Marx and Engels called themselves **communists** in 1847–48; what explanatory method and political claims the *Manifesto* actually made; and how Engels later reconstructed those ideas as **scientific socialism**.

## 1. Why “Communist” in 1847–48?

The immediate label in the *Manifesto* is not scientific socialist. It is **communist**.

Engels’s late-1847 draft, [*The Principles of Communism*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1847/11/prin-com.htm), opens by defining communism as “the doctrine of the conditions of the liberation of the proletariat.” The catechism proceeds from the historical origin of the modern proletariat in industrial capitalism, through private property and crisis, to communist political strategy. Engels then urged Marx to abandon the catechism format and turn the program into a manifesto.

The later 1888 English preface to the *Manifesto* explains the name retrospectively. Engels says that in 1847 “socialism” on the Continent was associated both with established utopian schools such as Owenism and Fourierism and with reformers who proposed remedies that did not threaten capital and profit. By contrast, the section of the working-class movement demanding fundamental social reconstruction called itself communist. Engels therefore summarized the contemporary distinction starkly: socialism was, in his retrospective formulation, a respectable middle-class movement; communism was associated with the working-class movement.

This retrospective account should not be mistaken for a neutral dictionary definition of Europe in 1847. It is Engels explaining the strategic self-understanding of his own current four decades later. But it is direct evidence for why he thought **Communist Manifesto** was the appropriate title.

The choice of “communist” was therefore already argumentative. Marx and Engels were locating their program inside an emerging proletarian movement and distancing it from socialist currents they regarded as paternal, sectarian, restorative, merely philanthropic, or detached from class politics.

## 2. What is distinctive in the Manifesto itself?

The *Manifesto* is polemic and political program, not a systematic treatise on scientific method. Its distinctiveness lies instead in the way it links historical explanation, class formation, capitalist development, and political action.

### Historical relations rather than timeless social forms

The first section, “Bourgeois and Proletarians,” narrates feudal and bourgeois social orders as historical formations rather than permanent arrangements. The bourgeoisie is not treated simply as a moral evil. Marx and Engels credit it with an immense revolutionary role: expanding world markets, transforming production, dissolving inherited relations, urbanizing society, and continually remaking social life.

That same development, they argue, generates the proletariat and intensifies the antagonism between wage labour and capital. The famous claim that the history of existing society is a history of class struggles is therefore not merely a slogan about inequality. It is an explanatory thesis about how social orders develop through changing relations among classes.

Engels’s 1883 and 1888 prefaces later identify this as the “basic thought” of the *Manifesto*: economic production and the social organization arising from it provide the basis for explaining political and intellectual history, while class struggle is the mechanism through which those historical antagonisms develop.

Modern scholarship is more cautious than Engels’s sweeping retrospective formulation. The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s discussion of Marx](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/marx/) emphasizes that Marx’s theory of history has to be reconstructed across several works and that interpretations of its causal structure remain contested. It is therefore better to say that the *Manifesto* is an important early political expression of Marx and Engels’s materialist conception of history than to treat it as a complete scientific theory of history.

### Communism as an immanent movement, not an invented ideal

Section II makes the methodological contrast especially clear. Marx and Engels say the communists do not establish sectarian principles with which to mould the proletarian movement. Their theoretical conclusions are presented instead as expressions of “actual relations” arising from an existing class struggle and historical movement.

That language is central to the later scientific-socialist self-description. The communist program is supposed to arise from an analysis of social relations already in motion rather than from a thinker designing the best society and persuading humanity to adopt it.

This does **not** mean the *Manifesto* contains no normative commitments or programmatic choices. It plainly does. It calls for the overthrow of bourgeois supremacy, the conquest of political power by the proletariat, and the abolition of bourgeois private property. It also lists transitional measures for advanced countries, including progressive taxation, abolition of inheritance, centralization of credit, public control of transport, expansion of state-owned productive capacity, and free public education.

The point is narrower: Marx and Engels claim that these aims correspond to a historically developing class movement rather than to a universal reformer’s invented social blueprint.

### A strong predictive rhetoric

The *Manifesto* also makes claims considerably stronger than a modest historical diagnosis. At the end of Section I, it declares the fall of the bourgeoisie and the victory of the proletariat “equally inevitable.”

That sentence is important because later descriptions of Marxism as scientific often blur two different meanings of “science”:

1. an attempt to explain social forms through material and historical conditions; and
2. confidence that this explanation yields determinate predictions about capitalism’s revolutionary outcome.

The *Manifesto* contains both impulses, but they should not be treated as identical. A materialist explanatory program can be evaluated separately from the pamphlet’s prophecy of proletarian victory. Later history also makes it impossible to present that prediction as an established empirical law.

Calling the *Manifesto* “scientific” should therefore describe a **self-conception and explanatory ambition**, not certify its forecasts as modern scientific findings.

## 3. Section III is the real bridge to the later utopian/scientific contrast

The inherited version of this report was right to emphasize Section III, “Socialist and Communist Literature,” but it flattened a more complicated taxonomy into a simple opposition between Marxism and naïve utopianism.

The *Manifesto* attacks several distinct rivals.

### Reactionary socialisms

Under “Reactionary Socialism,” Marx and Engels discuss **feudal socialism**, **petty-bourgeois socialism**, and German or “True” socialism. Their objection is not simply that these currents are insufficiently scientific.

Feudal socialism criticizes bourgeois society from the standpoint of a displaced aristocratic order. Petty-bourgeois socialism can identify real contradictions of modern production, but Marx and Engels argue that its positive program attempts to restore or preserve social relations being destroyed by capitalist development. “True” socialism is attacked for translating French socialist criticism into an abstract German philosophical idiom and detaching it from concrete political struggle.

The common accusation is historical mislocation: these currents criticize capitalism without recognizing, or without politically accepting, the class forces generated by capitalism itself.

### Conservative or bourgeois socialism

The *Manifesto* separately attacks reformers who want to remedy social grievances while preserving bourgeois society. Marx and Engels portray this as a desire for the advantages of bourgeois social relations without the antagonisms those relations generate.

This category is important because it shows that their boundary was not simply **revolutionary versus peaceful** or **socialist versus non-socialist**. Their deeper criterion was whether a doctrine accepted the underlying relations of production and class structure that communists meant to transform.

### Critical-utopian socialism and communism

The treatment of Saint-Simon, Fourier, Owen, and related currents is more ambivalent than a caricature of “utopians” suggests.

Marx and Engels grant these writers an important critical role. They recognize that the early socialist systems exposed genuine defects in existing society and generated valuable proposals and insights. The criticism is historical: these systems emerged when the proletariat and class struggle were still insufficiently developed to provide an independent political agent. Their founders therefore substituted designed models, exemplary communities, and appeals to society at large for an organized class movement.

The *Manifesto* argues that as capitalist industry and proletarian organization develop, the practical significance of these systems declines. That is the direct conceptual ancestor of Engels’s later opposition between **utopian socialism** and **scientific socialism**.

But the later label should not be inserted backward into the 1848 text as if Marx and Engels were already using it there.

## 4. What “scientific socialism” meant later

The explicit terminology becomes much clearer in Engels’s later work.

Engels wrote *Anti-Dühring* in 1876–78. At Paul Lafargue’s request, he reworked three chapters into a shorter French text published in 1880 as *Socialisme utopique et Socialisme scientifique*. Engels’s 1892 English preface recounts that publication history directly. Marx also supplied an introduction for the 1880 French edition and described the pamphlet as an introduction to scientific socialism.

In [Part III of *Socialism: Utopian and Scientific*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1880/soc-utop/ch03.htm), Engels presents what he calls the materialist conception of history. Social and political transformation is to be explained through changes in modes of production and exchange rather than through the discovery of eternal principles of justice. The task of socialist theory is to understand the historical conditions and contradictions that generate the modern proletarian movement.

This is where the later label becomes analytically precise. “Scientific” means, for Engels, that socialism should be grounded in an account of **historically produced social relations and their contradictions**, rather than in the construction of an ideal society from moral first principles.

It does not mean “scientific” in the narrow modern sense of controlled experimental science. Engels’s project combines historical explanation, political economy, dialectical philosophy, and revolutionary strategy. Its scientific status was itself a philosophical and political claim.

That distinction should remain visible. One can accurately describe Marx and Engels as aspiring to a science of capitalist society without treating every proposition they made about historical development as empirically verified.

## 5. The Manifesto’s relation to later scientific socialism

The relationship is best represented as continuity plus retrospective codification.

| 1847–48 | Later codification |
| --- | --- |
| **Communism** is the chosen political name. | **Scientific socialism** becomes an explicit theoretical label. |
| Engels’s *Principles of Communism* defines communism through the conditions of proletarian liberation. | *Anti-Dühring* and *Socialism: Utopian and Scientific* systematize the contrast with utopian socialism. |
| The *Manifesto* explains bourgeois society historically through production, class formation, and conflict. | Engels explicitly names the materialist conception of history as the basis of scientific socialism. |
| Communists claim to express an existing historical movement rather than invent sectarian principles. | Engels contrasts historically grounded socialist theory with systems derived from ideal schemes. |
| Section III criticizes rival socialist and communist literatures in their historical contexts. | The earlier critique is reorganized into the famous utopian/scientific opposition. |
| The pamphlet predicts bourgeois decline and proletarian victory in strongly deterministic language. | Later Marxist traditions debate how deterministic the materialist conception of history actually is. |

The left and right columns are related, but they are not interchangeable.

## 6. Why later prefaces matter

Readers often encounter the *Manifesto* through editions containing prefaces written decades after 1848. Those prefaces are invaluable, but they also create a risk: Engels’s mature vocabulary can silently become the vocabulary of the original pamphlet.

The 1872 German preface already treats the *Manifesto* as a historical document. Marx and Engels state that some details had become antiquated, especially in light of the experience of the Paris Commune, and they refuse to rewrite the old text to conceal those changes.

The 1883 and 1888 prefaces then make the materialist interpretation more explicit. Engels identifies the work’s basic historical proposition, explains the choice of “communist” over “socialist,” and situates the pamphlet in a longer history of working-class organization.

Those prefaces should therefore be read as **retrospective interpretation by an author**, not as transparent evidence that every later formulation was already present in finished form in 1848.

The distinction is especially important for “scientific socialism.” By 1890 Engels could refer retrospectively to the small 1848 “vanguard of scientific socialism.” That shows that he regarded the earlier movement as part of the later category. It does not show that the phrase was the movement’s operative public label when the *Manifesto* was written.

## 7. A corrected publication chronology

The inherited report also contained a basic translation error. It dated the first French translation to 1851. Marx and Engels’s own 1872 and 1888 prefaces give a different chronology.

- **November 1847:** the Communist League commissions Marx and Engels to prepare a theoretical and practical program.
- **Late 1847:** Engels drafts *The Principles of Communism* and recommends replacing the catechism with a manifesto.
- **February 1848:** the *Manifesto of the Communist Party* is first published in German in London.
- **Before the June 1848 insurrection:** a French translation appears in Paris.
- **1850:** Helen Macfarlane’s translation in the *Red Republican* becomes the first English translation.
- **1872:** Marx and Engels’s German preface explicitly says some practical details are antiquated but the document should not be rewritten retrospectively.
- **1876–78:** Engels publishes the material later excerpted from *Anti-Dühring*.
- **1880:** the French pamphlet *Socialisme utopique et Socialisme scientifique* appears.
- **1883–90:** Engels’s later prefaces increasingly frame the *Manifesto* through the mature materialist and scientific-socialist vocabulary.
- **1888:** Samuel Moore’s authorized English translation, revised with Engels, appears with Engels’s important English preface.

This is enough publication history for the argument. The inherited article’s sprawling claims about newspaper reactions, parliamentary scare tactics, and named “first” reviews were unevenly sourced and are not necessary to establish the conceptual history.

## 8. What should not be inferred from the phrase “scientific socialism”

Three cautions are necessary.

### It is not a claim that Marx and Engels were politically neutral observers

The *Manifesto* is a revolutionary political document commissioned by the Communist League. Its authors do not separate explanation from commitment. They describe capitalism, identify a historical agent, announce a program, and attempt to organize action.

### It is not a claim that the Manifesto’s predictions became laws of history

The pamphlet’s deterministic passages are part of the object being analyzed. They cannot be transformed into evidence merely by calling the underlying theory scientific.

### It is not a synonym for “everything Marx and Engels believed”

Marx and Engels revised arguments, responded to political experience, and wrote across several decades. The 1872 preface itself acknowledges that parts of the 1848 political program had become antiquated. A later systematic label should not erase development inside their own thought.

## 9. Why the inherited textual-network section was removed

The previous version supplied exact lemma frequencies and sentence-level co-occurrence counts for an English translation of the *Manifesto*, describing them as the output of a custom Python script. The package, however, contains no corpus snapshot, script, tokenizer/lemmatizer specification, stop list, generated data, or reproducible output.

That makes the precise counts editorially weak. They add an appearance of computational verification without preserving the materials required to reproduce the calculation.

The underlying qualitative observations—bourgeoisie/proletariat, class struggle, property, production, industry, revolution—are obvious and important, but they are better established by direct close reading than by unreproducible numbers.

The final article therefore removes the frequency and co-occurrence tables rather than laundering them into a new edition.

## 10. Final assessment

The inherited thesis can be repaired with one chronological distinction.

Marx and Engels entered 1848 as **communists**, not as authors publicly unveiling a doctrine under the title **scientific socialism**. Their program rejected invented social blueprints in favor of an account that claimed to arise from real historical relations, capitalist development, and proletarian class formation. Section III situated rival socialist currents according to their relation to that historical process. Those are precisely the features Engels later elevated into the opposition between utopian and scientific socialism.

So the *Manifesto* belongs to the genealogy of scientific socialism without having announced itself in those words.

That formulation is both stronger and more precise than saying Marx and Engels simply “dubbed” their 1848 doctrine scientific socialism. It preserves the genuine continuity in their thought while allowing later vocabulary to remain later.
