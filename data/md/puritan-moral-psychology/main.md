# Puritan Moral Psychology and the Genealogy of American Progressivism

## Executive Summary

Claims that modern American progressivism is “Puritanism without God” are too strong to survive historical scrutiny. New England Puritanism did establish influential forms of covenantal community, moral discipline, providential mission, public self-examination, and the jeremiad. Some of those forms were transmitted through later Protestant reform traditions, and later Americans repeatedly recovered and repurposed Puritan texts as resources for national self-description. But resemblance is not ancestry, and neither American progressivism nor modern administrative liberalism can be derived from a single seventeenth-century source.

A stronger genealogy distinguishes four different kinds of connection.

| Relationship | Evidentiary requirement | Puritan-to-progressive assessment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Direct transmission** | identifiable people, organizations, doctrines, texts, or institutional chains linking periods | strongest within Protestant religious reform, especially revivalism and the Social Gospel |
| **Reception and reuse** | later actors demonstrably read, cited, canonized, or repurposed Puritan texts or symbols | strong for twentieth-century uses of Winthrop and the “city upon a hill”; important precisely because much of this reception was late |
| **Morphological continuity** | recurring rhetorical or institutional form without demonstrated transmission | plausible for jeremiadic national self-criticism, reform-through-discipline, and exemplary mission |
| **Analogy** | structural resemblance without a traceable historical chain | insufficient to establish genealogy; this category includes many comparisons to contemporary bureaucratic or cultural practices |

The best-supported long-run claim is therefore narrower than the inherited thesis. **Puritan New England contributed one durable repertoire to American moral and political culture, especially covenant, jeremiad, disciplined moral community, and exemplary mission. A transformed Protestant reform lineage carried parts of that repertoire into nineteenth-century evangelical activism and the Social Gospel. Progressive-era reform then combined that religious inheritance with industrial crisis, labor conflict, social science, women’s reform networks, Black freedom traditions, republican and liberal political languages, and extensive transatlantic borrowing.**

Several findings materially limit a master Puritan genealogy.

First, Puritanism itself was diverse. Modern historians treat the term as useful but contextual and imprecise rather than a single psychological essence. John Coffey’s survey of “Puritan legacies” notes that sweeping theories crediting Puritanism with capitalism, liberal democracy, American exceptionalism, sexual repression, and other features of modernity have produced vigorous but inconclusive debates. Coffey recommends a more defensible method: trace how later generations actually received and used Puritan texts instead of assuming causal inheritance. [Coffey, “Puritan legacies,” 2008](https://doi.org/10.1017/CCOL9780521860888.020)

Second, the strongest bridge from older Reformed culture to reform politics runs through **later evangelical and Social Gospel Protestantism**, not through an unchanged Puritan psychology. Charles Finney’s revivalism made religious awakening a deliberately organized activity and was associated with antebellum social reform. By the late nineteenth century, Social Gospel thinkers explicitly redirected Christian salvation toward industrial and social conditions. This is real religious transmission, but it also involves substantial theological change. [McBride and Dorsey, 2023](https://doi.org/10.7591/cornell/9781501770531.003.0011); [Bowman, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1525/rac.2007.17.1.95)

Third, classic Progressivism was not a self-contained New England inheritance. Daniel T. Rodgers’s *Atlantic Crossings* documents extensive networks through which American reformers borrowed social insurance, urban planning, public housing, expert administration, and other policy ideas from Europe between the 1870s and the New Deal. Hull House itself was directly inspired by London’s Toynbee Hall. These transatlantic transfers make a purely national Puritan genealogy untenable. [Rodgers, *Atlantic Crossings*](https://history.princeton.edu/about/publications/atlantic-crossings-social-politics-progressive-age); [University of Chicago Library, Hull-House exhibition](https://www.lib.uchicago.edu/collex/exhibits/chicago-settlement-tradition-hull-house-centennial-exhibition/)

Fourth, even one of the most famous supposed continuous Puritan legacies—the national “city upon a hill” tradition—turns out to be partly a history of **retrospective canonization**. Abram Van Engen shows that Winthrop’s 1630 sermon was not continuously invoked as America’s founding text. It was published only in 1838 and became central to national political rhetoric largely after Perry Miller elevated it in the twentieth century. John F. Kennedy and later presidents then popularized it as an American-origin text. The episode demonstrates real Puritan influence, but through later scholarly and political reconstruction rather than uninterrupted cultural memory. [Van Engen, *City on a Hill*, 2020](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctvwcjf0t); [NEH, 2020](https://www.neh.gov/article/how-america-became-city-upon-hill)

Finally, American reform has other religious genealogies. African American prophetic traditions developed their own biblical languages of denunciation, covenant, Exodus, judgment, and national redemption. Treating civil-rights jeremiads as merely downstream of white New England Puritanism would erase a distinct and internally developed Black religious tradition. [Hobson, *The Mount of Vision*, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199895861.001.0001)

The defensible conclusion is not that Puritanism secretly became modern progressivism. It is that **Puritanism supplied one historically important moral vocabulary that later Protestants and national mythmakers repeatedly transformed, while progressive politics emerged from multiple religious, intellectual, social, and transnational lineages.** The strongest evidence supports transmission into Protestant reform and later reception in American national rhetoric. The weakest claims are those that infer direct descent from formal resemblance alone.

## 1. What a Genealogy Must Demonstrate

A genealogy is stronger than a comparison. Two institutions can resemble each other because they solve similar organizational problems, because both draw from a wider cultural tradition, or because one actually descends from the other. Only the last case is genealogy in the strict sense.

Historical claims about Puritan survivals are especially vulnerable to this problem. Public confession, moral discipline, surveillance, regulation, national mission, and campaigns against vice occur in many societies and traditions. Their presence in both seventeenth-century Massachusetts and a later reform movement cannot by itself prove that the later practice descended from the earlier one.

A usable method therefore asks four separate questions:

1. **Transmission:** Is there a traceable chain of texts, institutions, people, or traditions between the two periods?
2. **Reception:** Did later actors explicitly remember, cite, teach, canonize, or reinvent Puritan sources?
3. **Morphology:** Does a similar rhetorical or institutional form recur even when transmission cannot be shown?
4. **Counterfactual uniqueness:** Is Puritan ancestry needed to explain the later form, or could industrialization, Protestantism more broadly, republicanism, social science, transatlantic borrowing, racial politics, or another tradition account for it?

This method follows the direction suggested by contemporary Puritan historiography. Coffey notes that grand theories of Puritan modernity remain disputed and proposes studying later reception and representation as a more tractable form of legacy. The *Cambridge Companion to Puritanism* also stresses that “Puritanism” was never monolithic and is best treated as a contextual shorthand for diverse forms of early modern Reformed Protestantism. [Coffey and Lim, 2008](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-companion-to-puritanism/1825294AB20130B33EE2506DC63186AC)

The distinction changes the central question. Rather than asking whether progressives “are Puritans,” the useful questions are: **Which Puritan forms were actually transmitted? Which were recovered later? Which are only analogies? And how much explanatory work remains after competing genealogies are included?**

## 2. What Puritan New England Actually Contributed

### 2.1 Covenant and morally ordered community

John Winthrop’s *A Modell of Christian Charity* presents the Massachusetts migration as a covenanted collective project. Hierarchy and inequality are not absent from the sermon; they are incorporated into reciprocal obligations of mercy, stewardship, and mutual dependence. The community’s conduct has public meaning because covenantal failure would expose it to divine judgment before the world. [Winthrop, *A Modell of Christian Charity*](https://history.hanover.edu/courses/excerpts/212winth.html)

That is a strong primary-source basis for identifying several Puritan political forms: collective obligation, exemplary community, public consequences for moral failure, and the fusion of religious purpose with social organization.

### 2.2 Liberty inside moral authority

Winthrop’s 1645 speech commonly known as “On Liberty” distinguishes natural liberty from civil or federal liberty. Moral liberty exists under legitimate authority and is ordered toward what Winthrop calls the good, just, and honest. The point is not that later American reformers inherited this doctrine unchanged. It establishes that a prominent New England Puritan conception could interpret disciplined subjection to rightful authority as compatible with liberty rather than as its opposite. [Winthrop, “On Liberty”](https://teachingamericanhistory.org/document/on-liberty/)

### 2.3 Civil law and moral discipline

The Massachusetts Body of Liberties of 1641 joined protections that later readers recognized as important legal liberties with a religiously ordered civil regime. The State Library of Massachusetts describes it as the first legal code established by European colonists in New England and notes its later importance to Massachusetts statutory development. Its criminal provisions and surrounding legal order also demonstrate that the Puritan commonwealth did not separate religious orthodoxy, public morality, and civil governance in the modern liberal sense. [State Library of Massachusetts, “Massachusetts Body of Liberties”](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/massachusetts-body-of-liberties)

### 2.4 The jeremiad

The most influential scholarly account of a durable Puritan political rhetoric is Sacvan Bercovitch’s work on the American jeremiad. In Bercovitch’s interpretation, the jeremiad laments a community’s declension from its covenant while simultaneously renewing the covenant and its exemplary mission. Dissent can therefore operate not by abandoning the national project but by accusing the nation of betraying its own promised identity. [Bercovitch, *The American Jeremiad*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.36075995)

This is a powerful model of rhetorical continuity. It should not be inflated into a claim that all American moral criticism descends from Puritan sermons. Biblical prophecy, republican virtue, natural rights, abolitionism, Black Christianity, and other traditions can also generate condemnation followed by promised renewal. The jeremiad is best treated as one recurrent repertoire whose specific transmission must still be demonstrated.

## 3. From Puritanism to Evangelical Reform: A Real but Transformative Bridge

The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries do not preserve seventeenth-century Puritan theology intact. They transform it.

Revival Protestantism shifted emphasis toward conversion, popular mobilization, voluntary association, and practical techniques of religious awakening. Charles Grandison Finney is particularly useful because his *Lectures on Revivals of Religion* treated revival as something organized through intelligible means rather than simply awaited as an inscrutable divine event. A recent documentary history describes the work as an instruction manual for planning revivals and emphasizes Finney’s advocacy of deliberate revival meetings. [McBride and Dorsey, 2023](https://doi.org/10.7591/cornell/9781501770531.003.0011)

Finney’s milieu also connected evangelical religion to reform. George Marsden identifies Finney’s postmillennialism and antislavery commitments as examples of a period in which socially progressive reform was prominent in evangelical culture before the later fundamentalist “great reversal.” [Marsden, *Fundamentalism and American Culture*, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780197599488.003.0011)

Abolitionism provides a direct example of religious moral action entering national politics. The American Anti-Slavery Society’s 1833 declaration condemned slavery through both natural justice and Christian religion and explicitly sought to awaken public sentiment by appeals to conscience. [American Anti-Slavery Society, 1833](https://www.loc.gov/resource/rbpe.11801100/)

This chain is historically stronger than a simple leap from Winthrop to twentieth-century administration. Reformed and evangelical Protestant cultures shared scriptures, institutions, preaching traditions, conversion practices, reform networks, and moral vocabularies. But even here the transmission is not singular. The Second Great Awakening democratized religion, altered Calvinist assumptions, expanded voluntary organization, and generated reform movements with distinctive nineteenth-century conditions and ideologies.

The correct relationship is therefore **transformed Protestant continuity**, not unchanged Puritan psychology.

## 4. The Social Gospel: Religious Reform Meets Industrial Society

The Social Gospel is the strongest bridge between Protestant moral reform and the Progressive Era because it explicitly relocated Christian moral concern into industrial and social structures.

Historical scholarship describes the movement as a response to urbanization, industrial conflict, poverty, and the social consequences of capitalism. John Lee Eighmy summarizes the Social Gospel as a late nineteenth-century Protestant effort to apply Christian ethics to unprecedented problems of industrial society. [Eighmy, “Religious Liberalism in the South During the Progressive Era”](https://doi.org/10.2307/3163158)

Its theology was more than secular humanitarianism. Matthew Bowman shows that Social Gospel thinkers developed theological accounts in which sanctification and Christian conversion extended into economic and social life; the ideal Christian society erased a strict sacred/secular divide and imagined social transformation as part of religious renewal. [Bowman, “Sin, Spirituality, and Primitivism,” 2007](https://doi.org/10.1525/rac.2007.17.1.95)

This makes a genealogical connection to older Protestant moral community plausible. Yet the differences are equally important. Social Gospel theology was shaped by liberal Protestantism, postmillennial expectations, industrial capitalism, new social knowledge, and the specific crises of the Gilded Age. Scholarship also emphasizes internal diversity. Heath Carter notes that “social gospels” often flourished outside institutional churches and that religious liberalism and social progressivism could diverge. [Carter, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0009640715000049)

The movement should therefore be treated as a **mediating transformation**: it carried Christian moral languages into progressive reform, but the resulting politics cannot be read backward as a simple survival of New England Puritanism.

## 5. Why Classic Progressivism Cannot Be Reduced to Puritan Descent

### 5.1 Progressivism was institutionally and ideologically plural

The Progressive Era did not have one doctrine, one constituency, or one institutional program. Reformers pursued municipal government, regulation, labor law, public health, women’s suffrage, antitrust policy, social insurance, conservation, education, electoral reform, racial reform or exclusion, and other projects through different and sometimes conflicting languages.

David Brian Robertson’s account of Progressive-era social policy emphasizes the practical problem of building public capacity around constitutional limits, weak state administration, party patronage, and resistance from organized interests. Progressive reform produced new agencies and networks but also highly uneven provision divided by race and gender. [Robertson, “The Progressive Era,” 2013](https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780199838509.013.009)

A genealogy that treats all of this as a secularized Puritan moral project mistakes a heterogeneous political formation for a single creed.

### 5.2 Reform was transatlantic

The most damaging evidence against a self-contained Puritan genealogy is the documented international circulation of progressive policy.

Daniel T. Rodgers’s *Atlantic Crossings* reconstructs the networks through which American reformers studied and imported European experiments in social insurance, city planning, public housing, rural cooperation, administrative expertise, and other social policies from the 1870s through 1945. The book’s central contribution is precisely to challenge American-exceptionalist histories that explain reform entirely from domestic intellectual traditions. [Rodgers, *Atlantic Crossings*](https://history.princeton.edu/about/publications/atlantic-crossings-social-politics-progressive-age)

Settlement work provides a concrete institutional example. Jane Addams visited London’s Toynbee Hall in 1888 before founding Hull House in Chicago in 1889. The University of Chicago Library describes Toynbee Hall as a direct inspiration for the American settlement movement; later scholarship likewise treats settlement work as a transnational transfer whose London origins mattered to the movement’s form. [University of Chicago Library](https://www.lib.uchicago.edu/collex/exhibits/chicago-settlement-tradition-hull-house-centennial-exhibition/); [Reinders, 1982](https://doi.org/10.1086/643979)

Calling Hull House an “urban Puritan church without doctrine” therefore obscures more than it explains. It had religious and moral motivations, but its immediate institutional genealogy runs through British settlement reform, university extension, urban sociology, women’s networks, and the specific problems of industrial Chicago.

### 5.3 Religion mattered without monopolizing reform

Rejecting a Puritan master genealogy does not require secularizing Progressivism. Religion remained deeply important. Zoe Trodd’s survey of American social progressivism argues that religious belief and prophetic modes have repeatedly shaped reform movements and identifies abolition and anti-lynching activism as especially religious. [Trodd, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780199340378.013.462)

The more accurate conclusion is plural: **religious reform traditions were major contributors to Progressivism, but they interacted with social science, labor politics, women’s activism, racial politics, European policy models, and secular political languages.**

## 6. Puritan Reception Is Not the Same as Puritan Survival

The history of Winthrop’s “city upon a hill” is a useful test case because it reveals how a tradition can become influential through later reconstruction.

The familiar national story implies that Winthrop’s 1630 sermon continuously supplied Americans with an image of exceptional mission. Abram Van Engen’s history shows something more complicated. *A Modell of Christian Charity* was not published until 1838 and did not function continuously as the canonical origin text of the United States. Perry Miller’s twentieth-century scholarship elevated Winthrop’s sermon as a foundational expression of the American experience, after which political figures increasingly adopted it. [Van Engen, *City on a Hill*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctvwcjf0t)

The National Endowment for the Humanities summarizes the striking reception history: before Miller, American politicians had not made the sermon a standard origin text; after Miller, John F. Kennedy and numerous later presidents invoked it, while Ronald Reagan turned the image into the famous “shining city on a hill.” [NEH, 2020](https://www.neh.gov/article/how-america-became-city-upon-hill)

This does not make Puritan influence unreal. It changes its mechanism. A seventeenth-century text can become politically powerful because twentieth-century scholars, educators, anthologists, and politicians **recover and canonize it**. That is a history of reception, memory, and invented or renewed tradition—not proof that an intact Puritan psychology flowed continuously through three centuries.

The distinction strengthens rather than destroys the larger argument. American political actors repeatedly reach backward into a Puritan archive when constructing stories of mission, failure, renewal, and national purpose. What must be abandoned is the assumption that every later use reflects unconscious inherited continuity.

## 7. The Jeremiad Travels Through Multiple Traditions

Bercovitch’s jeremiad remains one of the strongest ways to identify a recurring American moral form: the community is accused of betraying its own founding promise, and denunciation becomes a demand for renewed fidelity rather than abandonment.

But the form does not belong exclusively to white Puritan descent. African American prophetic traditions developed powerful biblical languages of judgment, Exodus, covenant, apocalypse, and national redemption under conditions of slavery and racial domination. Christopher Hobson traces multiple African American prophetic traditions from the late eighteenth century through the twentieth, including reformist and apocalyptic strands that confronted the United States with divine judgment. [Hobson, *The Mount of Vision*](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199895861.001.0001)

Antebellum Black writers also actively reworked Pilgrim and Puritan origin narratives rather than simply inheriting them. Kenyon Gradert shows Black authors contesting and appropriating Pilgrim-Puritan national rhetoric as they negotiated American, Protestant, and Black identities. [Gradert, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/melus/mlz025)

This matters for later reform genealogy. A civil-rights speech that indicts America for violating its professed ideals may resemble a Puritan jeremiad, but it can also descend through Black prophetic Christianity, abolitionism, constitutional equality, and the lived politics of racial oppression. Reducing that rhetoric to Puritan survival would turn a shared and contested American form into a falsely singular lineage.

## 8. A Revised Genealogical Map

The evidence supports a branching model rather than a straight line.

| Period / formation | What can be traced | What should not be inferred |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New England Puritanism | covenantal community, moral discipline, providential mission, jeremiadic correction | a fixed transhistorical “Puritan personality” |
| Eighteenth-century revival culture | Reformed inheritance plus conversion, awakening, voluntary mobilization | unchanged Puritan Calvinism |
| Antebellum evangelical reform | organized moral reform, abolition, temperance, benevolence networks | one religious genealogy for all reform movements |
| Social Gospel | explicit Christian social salvation and institutional reform | proof that Progressive administration was primarily Puritan in origin |
| Classic Progressivism | religious reform + social science + labor/women’s movements + policy expertise + transatlantic borrowing | a single coherent ideology or purely American genealogy |
| Twentieth-century national rhetoric | demonstrable revival and reuse of Puritan symbols such as Winthrop’s sermon | uninterrupted popular memory from 1630 onward |
| Later administrative or cultural practices | possible analogies of discipline, confession, mission, or remediation | direct Puritan descent without transmission evidence |

This branching structure produces a more falsifiable thesis. Evidence for Puritan influence should increase when later actors can be connected to Protestant institutions, inherited texts, preaching traditions, explicit historical memory, or demonstrable reception. It should decrease when the same institution is better explained by European policy transfer, bureaucratic functional requirements, legal doctrine, racial politics, social science, labor conflict, or another religious tradition.

## 9. What Survives of the Original Thesis

Three parts of the original intuition survive the audit.

### 9.1 Moral reform is unusually important in American Protestant history

From Puritan covenant through revivalism, abolition, temperance, Social Gospel reform, and other movements, Protestant religious cultures repeatedly treated social conduct as a field of collective moral responsibility. That is a genuine historical continuity, although each stage transformed the theology and institutions it inherited.

### 9.2 Jeremiadic national self-criticism is a durable American repertoire

The pattern of accusing the nation of violating its own promise and demanding moral renewal has deep roots and has been repeatedly reused. Puritan preaching is an important source for understanding that repertoire, but later Black prophetic, republican, liberal, and other traditions also reshaped it.

### 9.3 Puritan symbols remain politically available because later generations rebuilt their importance

The “city upon a hill” case demonstrates that historical influence can be mediated by scholarship and memory. Puritan texts became resources for twentieth-century national identity partly because historians and politicians made them so. Reception is itself a causal mechanism.

What does **not** survive is the claim that modern progressivism can be read as a direct secularization sequence in which sin simply becomes injustice, church discipline becomes bureaucracy, confession becomes contemporary awareness practice, and Puritan mission becomes modern social policy. Those pairings may sometimes be suggestive comparisons, but without traceable transmission they are analogies, not genealogy.

## Conclusion

Puritanism belongs in the genealogy of American reform, but not at the top of a single family tree.

New England Puritanism institutionalized covenant, disciplined moral community, providential mission, and jeremiadic self-correction in forms that became historically important. Later Protestant movements transmitted and transformed parts of that inheritance. Revivalism made moral mobilization more voluntary and activist. Antebellum reform linked religious conscience to organized campaigns. The Social Gospel redirected Christian salvation toward industrial and social structures and entered direct conversation with Progressive reform.

At that point, however, the genealogy branches dramatically. Progressive politics also grew from industrial conflict, women’s organizing, Black freedom struggles, social science, constitutional and republican traditions, urban governance, and a dense North Atlantic market in reform ideas. Settlement houses and social policy cannot be understood without British and European precedents. Civil-rights moral rhetoric cannot be collapsed into white Puritan descent without erasing Black prophetic Christianity. Even Winthrop’s famous “city upon a hill” became a national master symbol through twentieth-century recovery and canonization rather than uninterrupted memory.

The strongest defensible formulation is therefore:

**Puritan New England supplied one influential moral-political repertoire within American culture. Its clearest descendants run through transformed Protestant reform traditions, while its later political importance also depends on deliberate reception and reinvention. American progressivism incorporated parts of that inheritance but arose from multiple domestic and transnational genealogies. Similarity to Puritan moral forms is evidence for comparison; only traceable transmission or documented reception is evidence for descent.**
