# The World Beneath the War: Material Reality in Westeros and Middle-earth

## Argument in brief

George R. R. Martin and J. R. R. Tolkien are often opposed as if one writes "realistic" fantasy and the other writes myth. That contrast is too crude. Both authors construct highly selective worlds. They differ less in whether they include enough technical detail than in **which parts of material reality they allow to become causally active**.

Martin's *A Song of Ice and Fire* is unusually strong at making material constraints enter political action. Armies consume food. War destroys harvests. Cities become vulnerable when supply routes break. Weather delays travel, kills people, and changes military possibilities. Famine is not merely scenery. Martin's famous question about "Aragorn's tax policy" belongs to this broader interest in the difficulty of rule: good intentions do not remove scarcity, unintended consequences, or administrative problems.

But Martin's realism is selective. The defining environmental premise of Westeros—seasons that last unpredictably for years—would transform settlement, agriculture, animal populations, fuel use, storage, demography, trade, and political institutions at almost every level. The novels acknowledge many of these effects, and Martin has explicitly described storage, fishing, glass gardens, false springs, migration, famine, and starvation. Yet the world does not model the full systemic consequences with the same intensity that it models dynastic politics and war.

Tolkien is selective in a different direction. Middle-earth is not an agronomic simulation. *The Lord of the Rings* does not give crop yields, hydrological budgets, or administrative ledgers. But landscape repeatedly possesses historical, moral, and narrative agency. Forests remember. Industrial violence alters land. The Shire can be wounded and restored. Isengard's transformation matters because the treatment of the nonhuman world is itself part of the political diagnosis.

The useful distinction is therefore not **realistic Martin versus romantic Tolkien**. It is:

> **Martin foregrounds material constraint where it intersects with conflict, survival, and rule; Tolkien foregrounds material place where it intersects with memory, stewardship, corruption, and belonging.**

Neither approach is a complete world-system. Both are powerful because they know which causal relations they want the reader to feel.

## Realism is causal integration, not inventory

A weak test of fantasy realism asks whether an author specifies enough facts:

- What is the soil pH?
- How many bushels of grain per acre?
- How much firewood does a city burn?
- What is the river's annual discharge?
- How many cattle survive a seven-year winter?

Those can be useful questions for a simulation, but their absence does not make a novel materially shallow. A fictional world can contain thousands of technical details that never constrain a character's choices. Conversely, a world can omit exact quantities while making material conditions decisive.

A better test is **causal integration**. A feature is deeply modeled when it changes the feasible action space of characters and institutions.

If food matters, armies must react to supply. If winter matters, stored energy and growing seasons must matter. If geography matters, travel and communication should bend around it. If forests matter, logging or destruction should transform more than scenery. If disease matters, it should alter labor, armies, households, or politics rather than merely provide atmosphere.

This produces five useful dimensions:

| Dimension | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Descriptive texture | Does the world feel materially particular? |
| Causal integration | Do material conditions change what can happen? |
| Institutional response | Do societies organize around recurring constraints? |
| System closure | Do consequences propagate beyond the immediate scene? |
| Symbolic load | Does the material world carry moral, historical, or metaphysical meaning? |

The inherited comparison confused these dimensions. Tolkien's landscapes are richly meaningful without being technically quantified. Martin's food and weather can be causally important without being modeled as a complete agricultural economy.

## What Martin actually meant by "Aragorn's tax policy"

Martin's famous remark is often flattened into a demand that fantasy novels contain tax tables. That was not his point.

In the 2014 Rolling Stone interview, Martin's argument was that **ruling is hard**. A morally good king still faces famine, war, fiscal choices, enemies, and unintended consequences. His challenge to Tolkien was less "show the tax rate" than "do not let moral legitimacy substitute for the actual problems of government." Contemporary discussion often remembers only the catchy tax-policy line, but the rest of the passage turns immediately to standing armies, flood and famine, surviving orcs, and decisions whose consequences can reverse the ruler's intentions. A recent discussion of the quotation and its medievalist assumptions is Liz Bourke's ["Aragorn's Tax Policy and Other Weird Shibboleths"](https://reactormag.com/aragorns-tax-policy-and-other-weird-shibboleths/).

That distinction matters because *A Song of Ice and Fire* does not itself supply a modern public-finance model of Westeros. Its political realism instead appears through **consequences**: rulers inherit debts, wars destroy productive capacity, food becomes leverage, refugees move, roads become dangerous, and apparently local decisions propagate through alliances and institutions.

Martin is strongest when institutions are forced to act under scarcity and imperfect knowledge.

## Food is not absent from Westeros

The inherited article badly understated Martin's treatment of food.

Food in *A Song of Ice and Fire* operates at several levels. Feasts signal class, region, abundance, and impending reversal. Armies forage and consume the countryside. War leaves villages burned and fields ruined. King's Landing becomes politically unstable as access to food deteriorates. The Reach matters not merely as a colored region on a map but as a major source of agricultural abundance. Winter stores become strategic resources rather than decorative cellar inventory.

Martin also addressed the North's winter economy directly in a 2001 exchange preserved in the Westeros.org [So Spake Martin archive](https://www.westeros.org/Citadel/SSM/Entry/The_Effects_of_Winter). Asked what northern populations eat during long winters, he described smoked and salted food, granaries, coastal and inland fishing, Winterfell's glass gardens, false springs, planting advice from maesters, movement south, famine, and starvation when winter outlasts storage.

That does not amount to a full agricultural model, but it decisively refutes the claim that winter is only an atmospheric plot device. Martin clearly intends winter to impose material constraints.

The stronger criticism is about **depth of propagation**. The world acknowledges storage and famine, but a civilization that has lived for millennia under unpredictable multi-year seasons would likely organize almost every major institution around this risk: settlement density, livestock strategies, forest management, preservation technology, interregional grain trade, emergency migration rights, inheritance patterns, marriage timing, fiscal reserves, military calendars, and demographic recovery after long winters.

The books model some of these channels. They do not systematically close the loop across all of them.

## The long seasons: fantasy cause, material consequences

Martin has repeatedly rejected a scientific explanation for Westeros's seasons. The premise is fantasy, not orbital mechanics. That is not a defect. A magical cause can have material effects just as a scientific cause can.

The relevant worldbuilding question is therefore not "Could Earth's axial tilt produce this?" It is "What does a society become after centuries of living with this?"

Real winter ecology illustrates why the consequences would be profound. Modern ecological research treats winter as a major driver of survival, energy balance, reproduction, phenology, community composition, and interactions among species. Reviews such as Williams, Henry, and Sinclair's ["Cold Truths"](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/brv.12105) and Contosta et al.'s ["Nine Maxims for the Ecology of Cold-Climate Winters"](https://academic.oup.com/bioscience/article/71/8/820/6213245) emphasize that winter cannot be understood as a blank interval between productive seasons. What happens in winter changes what becomes possible in the next growing season.

This evidence should not be used to "scientifically debunk" Westeros. It provides a **stress test** for causal depth. A five-year winter is not simply five ordinary winters placed back to back. It changes stocks of food, fuel, breeding animals, seed, human health, forest resources, transport, disease ecology, and the capacity to restart production.

Martin captures the existential direction of that process—"the food runs out"—but often leaves the intermediate social machinery implicit.

## War is Martin's strongest material system

Where Martin's world does achieve deep causal integration is war.

The series repeatedly makes political decisions inseparable from movement, provisioning, fortification, terrain, horses, roads, rivers, weather, local support, and the destruction of productive land. This is one reason Westeros feels materially denser than a setting in which armies simply appear wherever the plot needs them.

That strength should not be confused with literal medieval accuracy. Shiloh Carroll's [*Medievalism in A Song of Ice and Fire and Game of Thrones*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7722/j.ctt1wx91hk) emphasizes that the series constructs a modern image of the Middle Ages rather than transparently reproducing historical Europe. Brian Pavlac's edited [*Game of Thrones versus History*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/9781119249450) similarly treats Westeros as a mixture of historical inspiration and fantasy transformation.

But the causal instinct is real. Martin asks what war **does to the system around the warriors**. The answer includes burned crops, disrupted trade, refugees, ruined villages, food prices, predation by armed men, and administrative stress.

Historical research on medieval and early modern warfare reinforces why this focus matters. Logistics was not an incidental technical problem; armies had to procure, move, and protect food, animals, equipment, and money. John A. Lynn's [*Feeding Mars*](https://www.routledge.com/Feeding-Mars-Logistics-In-Western-Warfare-From-The-Middle-Ages-To-The-Present/Lynn/p/book/9780367157494) surveys the diversity of logistical systems rather than treating premodern armies as bodies that simply wandered and foraged without organization.

Martin's political realism is strongest when these material constraints force human choices.

## Geography works because it creates friction

Westeros's geography is not convincing merely because the map is large. It works when terrain changes strategy.

The Wall, mountain passes, river crossings, islands, coastlines, roads, castles, and long distances create bottlenecks and delays. Geography produces **friction**: it makes some alliances difficult, some invasions expensive, some fortresses strategically valuable, and some information stale by the time it arrives.

This is a more meaningful kind of realism than enumerating rock types. The reader does not need a geological survey of the Neck to understand that it constrains armies. Nor is a hydrological model of the Trident necessary for river crossings to matter.

The useful standard is again causal: a named river that never changes anything is decoration; a river that changes where armies can move is world structure.

## Where Martin remains abstract

The strongest criticisms of Martin's material world are therefore selective rather than comprehensive.

### Demography under multi-year seasons

Repeated long winters should produce strong demographic signatures. Population growth, childhood survival, age structure, marriage patterns, migration, and regional carrying capacity would all respond to recurrent famine risk. The novels dramatize hunger but rarely present societies whose demographic institutions visibly look as though they evolved under such extreme stochastic seasons.

### Energy and fuel

Food storage receives explicit attention. Fuel receives much less systemic treatment. Long cold periods would make woodlands, charcoal, peat or other energy stocks politically important, especially near castles and cities. Fuel scarcity could alter settlement patterns and forest governance even where food remained available.

### Livestock and seed continuity

Keeping humans alive is only part of surviving a prolonged winter. Agricultural recovery requires seed stocks, breeding populations, tools, draft animals, and labor. A society adapted to long winters would likely develop institutions specifically protecting these reproductive stocks. The books show stores and scarcity more readily than these deeper resilience mechanisms.

### Urban metabolism

King's Landing is politically vivid but materially selective. Food shortage becomes plot-relevant; sanitation, water, fuel, waste, and the continuous movement of bulk necessities receive less sustained causal treatment. This is not unique to Martin—most novels abstract urban metabolism—but it matters if the standard is full material simulation.

These are **limits of scope**, not proof that Westeros is badly built. Every fictional world abstracts. The critical question is whether the abstractions contradict the effects the narrative asks the reader to believe.

## Tolkien is not the opposite kind of spreadsheet

The inherited comparison made the symmetrical error with Tolkien. It treated Middle-earth as though Tolkien had supplied a more complete model of sustainable agriculture.

He did not.

The Shire has farms, gardens, orchards, mills, food traditions, and a strong agrarian identity, but *The Lord of the Rings* does not model crop rotations, yield distributions, soil chemistry, or demographic carrying capacity in a technical sense. Tolkien's material power lies elsewhere.

Landscape has **agency in the moral and historical architecture of the story**.

Fangorn is not timber inventory. Isengard's violence against trees is part of Saruman's political deformation. Mordor's damaged land manifests a mode of domination. The Scouring of the Shire matters because industrial and political corruption becomes visible as altered roads, buildings, water, trees, mills, and fields. Restoration is not merely a change of government; it is material repair.

Matthew Dickerson and Jonathan Evans's [*Ents, Elves, and Eriador: The Environmental Vision of J.R.R. Tolkien*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt2jctx1) develops this reading across Hobbit agriculture, Elvish aesthetics, Entish preservation, Mordor, Isengard, and the Scouring. Recent introductory scholarship likewise treats landscape, environment, flora, peoples, and political order as interconnected parts of Middle-earth; see Matthew Townend's 2024 Oxford chapter on [Middle-earth](https://academic.oup.com/book/58185/chapter-abstract/480528373).

Tolkien therefore integrates material reality primarily through **meaning, memory, and stewardship**, not technical administrative closure.

## Tolkien's ecology is causal even when it is mythic

Calling Tolkien's environment "symbolic" can sound like saying it is less real. In the narrative, the opposite is often true. The symbolic dimension makes the environment more causally important.

Saruman's industrialization of Isengard does not remain a metaphor. It changes the physical environment and helps generate the forces that destroy his power. The Ents act. The Shire's degradation changes the home to which the Hobbits return and forces them to exercise capacities acquired during the larger war. Sam's restoration is both social and ecological.

Ecocritical scholarship has long emphasized that Tolkien's nonhuman world is not simply passive scenery. Ann M. Martinez's chapter on [the green medievalism of Tolkien's Elven realms](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/studies-in-medievalism-xxvi/elvencentrism-the-green-medievalism-of-tolkiens-elven-realms/1AEA33089EE051339F9CD17B766032D4) connects Tolkien's landscape ethics to care for nonhuman nature. Dickerson and Evans similarly treat agriculture, horticulture, forests, conservation, and restoration as components of a broader environmental vision.

This is a different form of material causality from Martin's grain stores and marching armies, but it is not a weaker form simply because it is mythic.

## The comparison that survives

The two authors can now be compared on dimensions that actually matter:

| Dimension | Martin / Westeros | Tolkien / Middle-earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Political decision under scarcity | very high | usually secondary to the quest and moral order |
| War logistics and movement | high | present but less administratively foregrounded |
| Food and famine | recurrent causal pressure | present, especially through home, provisioning, hospitality, and devastation, but less systematized |
| Long-run ecological system closure | selective; extreme seasons create unresolved institutional questions | selective; ecology is not a quantitative simulation |
| Landscape as historical actor | moderate to high in particular regions | very high |
| Landscape as moral actor | present, especially through winter and devastation | central |
| Administrative detail | high relative to epic fantasy, but still selective | comparatively low |
| Symbolic ecology | seasons, ice/fire, ruined lands | forests, gardens, wastelands, stewardship, industrial destruction |
| Main realism effect | consequences of power among constrained humans | meaningful inhabitation of a morally and historically charged world |

Neither column is "more realistic" in every sense. They create different kinds of density.

## Martin's critique of Tolkien partly rebounds—but not completely

The "Aragorn's tax policy" criticism does rebound on Martin in one important way. Once an author explicitly values downstream consequences of rule, readers are justified in asking whether the author's own largest premises propagate far enough.

Westeros's long seasons are an obvious test. The books do better than the inherited article admitted: winter storage, fishing, false springs, famine, and migration are all acknowledged. But the full civilization-wide adaptation to radically unpredictable growing seasons remains only partly visible.

That is a fair asymmetry to point out.

But it does **not** follow that Martin fails by his own standard because he never specifies taxes, soil pH, aqueduct capacity, or the caloric content of grain stores. His stated standard was not encyclopedic completion. It was that rule and violence have consequences, intentions can fail, and political morality does not erase difficult material choices.

On that narrower standard, *A Song of Ice and Fire* very often succeeds.

## A better standard for worldbuilding

The comparison suggests a more useful framework for evaluating constructed worlds.

### 1. Choose the material variables that matter

A story does not need to model everything. It should know which environmental and infrastructural variables its major conflicts depend on.

### 2. Make those variables constrain action

If winter is important, it should change food, movement, mortality, and planning. If forests are important, their destruction should alter more than background art.

### 3. Let consequences propagate at least one level beyond the scene

A burned field should not only look tragic; it should affect supply, prices, migration, or military capacity if the story remains in that region long enough.

### 4. Model institutions as adaptations to recurring problems

If a problem has existed for centuries, societies should have customs, offices, technologies, reserves, routes, rituals, or laws designed around it. This is where Westeros's long seasons invite the hardest questions.

### 5. Do not confuse detail with causality

A paragraph of agricultural terminology can be less convincing than one well-placed shortage that reshapes a war.

### 6. Do not confuse symbolism with unreality

Tolkien's forests can be morally charged and materially causal at the same time. Fantasy does not become less real because the environment carries metaphysical meaning.

## Conclusion

George R. R. Martin did not build a politically dynamic world on a static natural backdrop. That verdict is too severe and, in places, simply contradicted by the text and his own explanations. Food, weather, geography, transport, war damage, storage, and famine repeatedly constrain action in Westeros.

Nor did Tolkien build a more complete ecological simulation. Middle-earth's material strength is not technical closure. It is the integration of landscape with memory, culture, corruption, restoration, and moral order.

The deeper difference is one of **causal emphasis**.

Martin's world becomes most materially real when bodies, armies, households, and rulers run into scarcity. Tolkien's becomes most materially real when inhabitants reveal what kind of beings they are through the way they dwell in, exploit, defend, damage, or restore places.

Martin's famous demand for consequences remains valuable. It can even be turned back on Westeros, especially against the civilizational implications of its long seasons. But the strongest version of that critique is not "Where is the soil pH?" It is:

> **If this material condition has shaped the world for centuries, where are the institutions that learned to live with it?**

That question is useful far beyond either author. It distinguishes a world containing details from a world whose details have had time to become history.
